Robots predict many action coordinates, so evaluation scores can disagree. For a diffusion policy, we separate the quantity explained by Integrated Gradients, which uses numerical integration, from the response scoring input removal and restoration. Q is negative squared distance from the reference action; N is negative distance with a small stabilizer. An exact identity shows that, for the same curve, N's normalized area never exceeds Q's when partial input replacement moves predictions no farther than replacing every feature of the tested input type. Larger changes, called overshoots, can reverse this ordering. In saved simulated cube-picking runs of a 170-million-parameter model with no successful episodes, all medians of Q-minus-N differences are positive, but extreme calls make most means negative. For image-patch deletion in three matched groups (130 episodes), N-target Integrated Gradients exceeds Q-target under both responses in pretrained PickCube and StackCube; both beat random under N in all three, but under Q only in StackCube. The raw-mean winner is the method maximizing mean advantage over random ordering. Switching Q and N changes it in 80% of pretrained PickCube deletion resamples, whereas scaled winners match in 90% and 98% for pretrained and task-trained PickCube. Only 1 of 12 image-patch comparisons statistically separates the top two. Checks on twelve matched contexts of one pretrained model show 16-bit versus 32-bit calculations and integration rule affect attribution recovery. These samples demonstrate possible numerical failures, not their frequency, and do not verify the original rankings; response choice, ranking quality and numerical accuracy therefore require separate evidence.
