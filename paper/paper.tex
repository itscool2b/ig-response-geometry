\begin{abstract}
Feature attribution evaluation depends on the response used to score predictions after inputs are removed or restored. We separate the attribution target from the scoring response for a robot diffusion policy, comparing squared action discrepancy (Q) with stabilized distance (N). An exact identity shows that N cannot increase insertion or deletion area under the curve (AUC) when interventions remain within the endpoint range. Overshoots, which move the prediction farther from the reference action than replacing every feature with its baseline, can reverse this effect. Saved PickCube cohorts contain no successful episodes; their eight paired median Q-minus-N differences range from 0.104 to 0.159, while extreme calls reverse six means. Historical RDT-1B rescoring gives positive vision medians in successful and failed groups. A new experiment preserves inputs for 281 contexts and compares seven rankings with matched random and fixed orders. The preferred vision-deletion method changes between responses in all three new banks; only one cell has an adjusted best-minus-second interval excluding zero. Numerical comparisons separate completeness, coordinate error, ranking stability and AUC sensitivity. Increasing integration budgets reduces fp32 residuals but leaves a bf16-forward floor; no nonfinite value occurs at 815,210 evaluated nodes. These limited context samples do not estimate general failure rates or verify the original saved rankings. Response calibration can change practical comparisons, and numerical checks require separate interpretation from ranking quality.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} integrated gradients, attribution evaluation, diffusion policies, perturbation metrics, numerical integration, robotics

\section{Introduction}

Diffusion policies generate action sequences by denoising predictions conditioned on observations\citep{chi2023diffusion}. Their vector outputs require a scalar function for feature attribution or perturbation evaluation\citep{zhang2026embodied}. Integrated Gradients (IG) integrates sensitivity along a baseline-to-input path\citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}; we study this procedure with the Robotics Diffusion Transformer (RDT)\citep{liu2024rdt}. The function being attributed and the response scoring subsequent interventions are distinct choices.

Insertion and deletion tests progressively restore or remove ranked features\citep{petsiuk2018rise}. Retraining after removal\citep{hooker2019roar}, information exposed by masks\citep{rong2022road} and disagreeing perturbation metrics\citep{tomsett2020sanity} complicate interpretation. Removal frameworks distinguish the intervention, model behavior and summary\citep{covert2021explaining}. Artificial inputs can leave the training distribution\citep{hase2021ood}; insertion/deletion AUC also discards attribution magnitudes once their ranking is fixed\citep{gomez2022metrics}. Meta-evaluation tests metric reliability\citep{hedstrom2023metaquantus}, while occlusion strategy can change method rankings\citep{blucher2024decoupling}. Baseline choice similarly changes the question answered in regression\citep{mamalakis2023baseline}.

Policy explanations have additional interpretation problems. Counterfactual tests challenge saliency-based hypotheses in reinforcement learning\citep{atrey2020exploratory}; evaluating action preference differs from evaluating state value\citep{huber2022benchmarking}. Input attribution has been adapted to robotic torque outputs\citep{kim2021explaining}, and internal activation steering can change vision-language-action behavior\citep{haon2025mechanistic}. These studies motivate specifying the behavioral quantity before interpreting a score.

Hama, Mase and Owen derive interaction-dependent random-order references for unnormalized insertion/deletion areas, attributing and evaluating one scalar function\citep{hama2023deletion}. We instead separate target and response for vector action discrepancies, derive an endpoint-normalized identity, investigate overshoots, and compare calibrated rankings in matched robot contexts. Changing the response alone can move a deletion median across an illustrative bar of 0.40; this bar is not an empirically validated decision rule.

Numerical recovery is another question. IG's axiomatic conditions require care\citep{lundstrom2022rigorous}, and completeness does not guarantee usefulness for a chosen end-task\citep{bilodeau2024impossibility}. Exact integration for piecewise-linear networks exposes errors in sampling approximations\citep{sotoudeh2019linear}; ground-truth benchmarks supply a different evaluation criterion\citep{arras2022clevrxai}. Earlier studies examine budget-dependent completeness\citep{makino2024integration} and modify integration steps to reduce explanation noise\citep{yang2023idgi}. We distinguish accurate recovery of a specified integral, stable rankings and favorable perturbation scores.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Research design and provenance}

The original evidence comprises two unsuccessful PickCube cohorts, each with \RescoreEpisodes{} episodes and \RescoreCalls{} calls, ranked separately using Q and N. The model was recorded as the released RDT-170M\citep{rdt170mrelease}. Shared environment-seed blocks do not make these cohorts matched: vision baseline residuals differ in every aligned call, and their log correlation falls from \CohortInitialCorrelation{} at call 0 to 0.289 at call 2. Original observations and diffusion-noise arrays are unavailable. Rescoring reproduces their recorded response changes, not the original policy executions.

We additionally rescore saved RDT-1B PickCube runs and RDT-170M PegInsertionSide and PickSingleYCB runs. The new R1 experiment contains P (PickCube, RDT-170M: 60 episodes, 120 contexts), B1 (PickCube, authors' RDT-1B checkpoint: 40, 80), S (StackCube, RDT-170M: 30, 60), and 21 preserved Month 8 contexts. The new banks uniformly select two executed calls per episode under a rule locked before collection, while preserving every executed call. Table~\ref{tab:r1-experiments} separates all experiment sources and estimators; Table~\ref{tab:r1-identities} summarizes provenance. Full hashes, settings and status evidence accompany the archive.

\subsection{Targets, features and numerical estimators}

Fix context $c$, model, noise and other modalities. Let $g(u)$ predict the active action chunk when one modality is replaced by $u$; $x$ is its actual input, $x'$ its baseline and $a=g(x)$ the fixed reference. The chunk has 64 timesteps and eight active action coordinates, so $d_a=512$. ManiSkill3\citep{tao2024maniskill3} executes every fourth action, up to 16 steps per call and 400 steps per episode. Define
\begin{equation}
 r(u)=\|a-g(u)\|_2^2,\qquad Q(u)=-\frac{r(u)}{2d_a},\qquad N(u)=-\sqrt{r(u)+\epsilon},\quad\epsilon=10^{-12}.
\label{eq:targets}
\end{equation}
Q is an auxiliary discrepancy, not a policy log likelihood. The reference is computed once with fixed noise. Figure~\ref{fig:r1-design} separates attribution from scoring.

\label{fig:r1-design}

Vision groups are 729 adapted patch tokens from one external image; five other camera slots remain fixed backgrounds. Replacement uses matching adapted tokens from a gray image. SigLIP\citep{zhai2023siglip} and T5-XXL\citep{raffel2020t5} supply image and language representations. Language attribution operates after the adaptor over attended token positions; replacement does not rerun the encoder. Q-ranked historical runs used encoded padding/end-of-sequence tokens; N-ranked runs used the authors' precomputed embedding with a zeroed placeholder baseline. State groups are eight active normalized coordinates, differentiated through the state adaptor against zero. Baselines are comparisons, not unique neutral inputs\citep{sturmfels2020visualizing}; representation-space paths need not encode valid language\citep{sanyal2021discretized}.

For target $F$, IG and the historical endpoint-inclusive estimator are
\begin{equation}
 A_i^F=(x_i-x_i')\int_0^1 f_i(\alpha)\,d\alpha,\qquad
 E_{i,m}=\frac{x_i-x_i'}{m+1}\sum_{j=0}^{m}f_i(j/m),
\label{eq:ig}
\end{equation}
where $f_i(\alpha)=\partial_i F(x'+\alpha(x-x'))$. The trapezoidal estimate $T_{i,m}$ gives half weight to endpoints\citep{trefethen2014trapezoidal}; exactly,
\begin{equation}
 E_{i,m}=\frac{mT_{i,m}+(x_i-x_i')[f_i(0)+f_i(1)]/2}{m+1}.
\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
This endpoint term can cause first-order error. Historical $m=64$ bf16 runs replaced nonfinite gradients with zero without counting them. The numerical accuracy of the saved headline rankings is unverified.

R1 compares the historical kernel A0; bf16 forward/fp32 accumulation A1; fp32 forward A2; Gauss-Legendre A3 with 32, 64 and 128 nodes; and adaptive Gauss-Kronrod A4. A1/A2 share 513 nodes, yielding nested trapezoidal and endpoint-inclusive rules through 512 intervals and midpoint rules through 256. A2 covers 12 P contexts and the 21 preserved contexts. Nonfinite values are counted at every evaluated node, never replaced. A1 T512 is a finite-budget comparator. An fp32 reference is qualified only when A4 converges and A4, GL128 and A2 T512 agree pairwise within relative L1 error $10^{-3}$.

\subsection{Interventions, calibration and statistics}

Insertion restores groups from baseline; deletion replaces groups in the actual input. Group scores sum absolute attribution coordinates; mean path gradients omit the IG input-difference factor. With $B=r(x')>0$, normalization and the paired response difference are
\begin{equation}
 C_S(u)=\frac{S(u)-S(x')}{S(x)-S(x')},\qquad
 \Delta=\mathrm{AUC}_Q-\mathrm{AUC}_N.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
An overshoot moves the prediction farther from the reference action than the all-baseline input, so $r>B$. Normalization is undefined for $B=0$. AUC uses trapezoidal weights over intervention fractions; larger insertion and smaller deletion areas are conventionally favored.

The saved nominal schedule is 0, 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75 and 100\%. Historical intermediate group counts use $k=\max(1,\mathrm{round}(Gp/100))$, with ties rounded to even. Pinned tokenization gives G=20, 30, 25 and 16 for PickCube, StackCube, PegInsertionSide and PickSingleYCB. Thus 1\% and 5\% select one group for G=16,20,25; all 3,000 headline language curves have equal saved responses there. No such equality occurs in the 800 StackCube curves. This recovers the rule, not missing historical masks. Saved analyses retain nominal weights; R1 uses preserved masks and realized fractions, with nominal-axis sensitivity results.

R1 ranks seven methods: Q/N IG and Q/N mean path gradient (PG) at A1 T512, input difference, and historical Q/N IG at A0m64. Controls are 32 random vision orders, 64 language orders and the exact random-order average for eight state groups, plus raster/reverse/centre-out vision orders, forward/reverse token orders and forward/reverse joint orders. Advantage is method-minus-random AUC for insertion and random-minus-method for deletion. Preferred means highest episode-mean advantage on common complete cases. Subtracting one common reference cannot reorder methods within a response. We therefore compare response choice, random-SD z-scaling, population and illustrative 0.4/0.5 deletion bars separately.

Episode percentile bootstrap uses 10,000 draws. Seven primary P vision-deletion contrasts use Bonferroni alpha 0.05/7; best-minus-second intervals use 0.05/4 across response/direction cells. Other intervals are descriptive. Best-method ties remain undetermined. Coverage was simulated before unblinding. The plan and amendments precede their governed results. Historical rescoring intervals use seed 0 and R1 seed 930011; neither captures training-run, checkpoint or wider task variation. We retain all finite tails and all eight dependent headline cases; emphasis on medians and positive shares was chosen retrospectively.

\subsection{Diagnostic scope and ethics}

Completeness measures $|\sum_iA_i-[F(x)-F(x')]|/|F(x)-F(x')|$; coordinate change compares relative L1 maps, and ranking comparisons use Spearman, top-group overlap and intervention-mask Jaccard. We separately recompute normalized-AUC changes with one common scoring forward. Zero denominators remain undefined. The 1\% completeness and 0.01 AUC-change criteria are study-specific diagnostics, not accuracy guarantees. RMS discrepancy is also retained in physical response units; its unstabilized normalized area equals N's unstabilized area and supplies no independent normalized metric.

The earlier offline fp32 sample is separate: 12 completed RDT-170M contexts per modality and target, six episodes across four tasks, no completed RDT-1B context. Its cached function and budget comparisons demonstrate possible failures, not their prevalence in the original rankings or diffusion policies generally. Full check results remain archived. All experiments use simulation; no human or animal subjects were involved and no IRB approval was required.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Response identity and saved-curve effects}

Under exact self-reference, Equation~\ref{eq:normalization} gives
\begin{equation}
 C_Q-C_N=\frac{(\sqrt{r+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon)(\sqrt{B+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon})}{B}.
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
The difference is positive for $0<r<B$, zero at endpoints and negative at overshoots. Nonnegative AUC weights therefore imply $\Delta\ge0$ for fully in-range curves, strictly when an interior residual receives positive weight. Lowering AUC favors deletion but disfavors insertion for the same ranking. The pointwise upper bound is 1/4; endpoint weights tighten the saved-schedule bound to 0.2175. These statements do not depend on IG accuracy.

For an additive equal-feature policy, Q introduces interactions through squaring. At 100 features, every ordering gives AUC 0.65976 on the saved schedule, versus 0.5 for unstabilized N. These are analytic reference values, not measured RDT controls. Hama's interaction-dependent expectation explains why a universal 0.5 reference is inappropriate; 0.40 remains illustrative.

The eight positive paired medians, \PairedMedianMin{} to \PairedMedianMax{}, quantify a response shift whose in-range direction was already predicted. Positive shares range from \PositiveShareMin{}\% to \PositiveShareMax{}\%, while overshoots occur in \OvershootShareMin{}\% to \OvershootShareMax{}\% of calls (Figure~\ref{fig:r1-saved-geometry}; Table~\ref{tab:r1-saved-summary}). Six means are negative. Q-ranked vision insertion has mean $-17.10$ but median 0.104; first calls contribute $-16.92$ on the same 750-call denominator. Neither cohort establishes ranking superiority.

\subsection{Numerical accuracy and separate diagnostic outcomes}

For the reviewer's linear example $g(u)=u$, baseline 0 and input 1, exact N attribution is approximately 0.999999. At $m=64$, endpoint-inclusive averaging gives 0.984615 (1.54\% shortfall), trapezoidal integration 0.992187, midpoint 0.99999999984 and Gauss-Legendre 0.9999999958. The narrow endpoint transition explains why treating endpoints equally matters even with exact gradients.

Historical saved vision completeness residual medians are 1.96\% for Q-ranked and 4.76\% for N-ranked calls; 77.2\% and 99.6\% exceed 1\%. Sums fall short of endpoint gaps in 87.5\% and 99.1\%. Every stored sum is bf16-valued, but storage rounding alone cannot explain the one-sided deficit.

In P vision, increasing trapezoidal intervals from 64 to 512 changes A1 bf16-forward median residuals only from 0.0403 to 0.0351 for N and 0.00865 to 0.00834 for Q. A2 fp32 values fall from 0.0154 to 0.00192 and $2.61\times10^{-4}$ to $3.21\times10^{-6}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:r1-numerics}). The same floor-versus-decrease pattern holds on the matched 12 contexts. Historical maps differ from the bf16 T512 comparator by median relative L1 0.0207 (N) and 0.0182 (Q); against fp32 T512 the corresponding 12-context medians are 0.0603 and 0.0321. Forward precision, not simply adding nodes, therefore limits these bf16 comparisons.

Ranking stability does not remove score sensitivity. For A0 versus A1 T512 on 120 P vision contexts, median group Spearman is about 0.999975 under both targets and median top-5\% overlap is 1. Nevertheless, N-target deletion AUC changes exceed 0.01 in seven contexts scored with N and 12 scored with Q; Q-target counts are four and 12. Maximum Q-scored deletion change for the Q-target comparison is 221.03. All four diagnostics therefore require separate reporting.

No NaN or infinity occurs at 815,210 evaluated nodes. A4 converges in all 48 reference comparisons, but strict three-way qualification holds for only 5 of 24 N comparisons; convergence alone does not establish the reference. A0 residuals depend on GPU summation order, so the per-context fp32-sum alternative is retained. No fp64 model run was performed. The earlier separate offline fp32 sample likewise shows that repeated equality checks can pass while budget comparisons fail; its limited sample is not a failure-rate estimate. The short appendix gives constructed exact-ranking reversal and aliasing examples, not observed robot-policy reversals.

\section{Discussion}

Our analytic result isolates a scoring effect that persists with unchanged actions and arbitrary rankings. The saved unsuccessful runs quantify its size and tails; successful 1B episodes and extra tasks broaden the observed scope without implying universal effect sizes. The matched R1 study answers a different practical question: changing the response can change the preferred method after response-matched calibration. Most best-versus-second intervals still include zero, so selecting the highest mean is weaker evidence than establishing a clear winner.

Hama's random-order result concerns feature interactions and unnormalized full-prefix areas. Their sufficient condition uses a strictly increasing differentiable transform of an additive inner model on binary features. Its IG argument extends the inner model affinely along the path, so gradients have a common coordinate order when $h'>0$. Our construction has a varying path-gradient direction and does not satisfy that condition. Our additional findings concern separate targets and responses, endpoint normalization, overshoots and numerical recovery in a vector-output policy. They do not replace general interaction results. Compared with intervention and metric studies, we hold interventions fixed to isolate the response. Compared with exact-integration work, our finite diagnostics expose limitations without certifying coordinates. No model-weight randomization study was performed\citep{adebayo2018sanity}.

A practical evaluation should specify target, response, reference, noise, groups, baselines and masks; compare rankings in identical contexts with response-matched random and fixed controls; and report undefined gaps, unnormalized residuals, medians and full tails alongside means. Episode-level resampling respects repeated calls. Completeness, coordinate convergence, rank stability and perturbation-score stability should be reported separately across budgets and precisions. Passing a finite set of checks does not guarantee a correct integral or improved robot behavior.

\subsection{Limitations and conclusion}

The numerical accuracy of the saved headline rankings is unverified. Their missing original inputs cannot be restored by R1's regeneration success. Historical cohorts are not matched ranking comparisons; all original headline episodes, P and S fail. Successful evidence rests mainly on historical 1B and B1, with differing seeds, language inputs and runtimes. Month 8 samples are too small for population inference, and partially replaced inputs have no demonstrated causal explanation here. The separate offline fp32 sample and R1 subsets show what can occur, not general numerical failure frequencies.

The mathematical identity establishes the direction of fully in-range response changes. Saved and new experiments establish conditional effects in their recorded contexts, including successful 1B episodes and response-dependent preferred methods. Numerical comparisons and counterexamples establish limits on particular estimators and checks. These conclusions justify explicit response calibration and qualified numerical reporting, not general ranking superiority or improved task success.

\section{Short analytic proofs}

Set $\eta=\sqrt\epsilon$, $b=\sqrt{B+\epsilon}$ and $z=\sqrt{r+\epsilon}$. Because $B=(b-\eta)(b+\eta)$, subtracting the normalized responses gives $(z-\eta)(b-z)/B$, proving Equation~\ref{eq:identity}. In range its maximum is $(b-\eta)/[4(b+\eta)]\le1/4$. Beyond range it is negative. The saved schedule gives zero endpoint differences total weight 0.13, hence $\Delta\le0.87/4=0.2175$. If B=0 normalization is undefined. Equal saved 1\%/5\% values make deletion of the 1\% node change their combined weight by 0.02, bounding its in-range paired effect by 0.005.

For scalar action examples set $d_a=1$. With $g(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+(4/5)x_2$, baseline (0,0), input (1,1) and reference 9/5, polynomial integration gives $A^Q=(23/30,64/75)$. Writing $\rho(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha+4/5)$, N's gradient is $\rho(\alpha)(2\alpha,4/5)/\sqrt{\rho(\alpha)^2+\epsilon}$. The inequality $\rho\ge(4/5)(1-\alpha)$ bounds the coordinate deficits from (1,4/5) by $2.5\times10^{-6}$ and $10^{-6}$ at the chosen epsilon. Thus N ranks feature 1 first while Q ranks feature 2 first. This is a constructed possibility, not an observed PickCube reversal.
