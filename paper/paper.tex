\begin{abstract}
Attribution evaluation depends on the ranking that selects interventions and the response used to score them.
We study this distinction for Integrated Gradients (IG) of a fixed-noise diffusion policy's predicted action chunk.
For quadratic and stabilized negative-$\ell_2$ action-discrepancy targets, we derive an exact relation between the normalized responses and a positive, path-dependent factor between their gradients.
A smooth construction shows that the IG rankings can reverse even when the nonzero gradients are pointwise collinear.
A finite-grid example illustrates the response dependence of random-ranking AUC.
We examine the practical effect by reanalyzing \ArchiveFiles{} preserved Robotics Diffusion Transformer files.
Changing only the response changes median vision deletion AUC from \RescoreQDeletionQ{} to \RescoreQDeletionL{} while the recorded quadratic-target rankings stay the same.
Across eight paired rescoring cases, every median response change is positive, but overshooting tails make six means negative.
Exploratory episode-omission checks leave the insertion-mean signs unchanged, although the language-deletion signs can reverse.
A second smooth construction shows that several integration grids can agree in completeness, ranking and perturbation curves while reversing the exact IG order.
In this example, the inaccurate order even scores better on both perturbation metrics.
Existing actual-model diagnostics also show why repeatability and integration-budget sensitivity must be considered separately, although the cohort is incomplete and the sensitivity remains unresolved.
These results separate the roles of response geometry, numerical accuracy and ranking quality.
They do not qualify a production numerical setting, demonstrate superior attribution rankings, or explain task success or learned-weight dependence.
The artifact preserves the raw-record lineage, analytic checks, adverse results and provenance limits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) models such as RT-2~\citep{zitkovich2023rt2}, Octo~\citep{team2024octo}, and the Robotics Diffusion Transformer (RDT)~\citep{liu2024rdt} use sensory observations and instructions to predict robot actions.
Diffusion policies~\citep{chi2023diffusion} produce action chunks through iterative denoising~\citep{ho2020ddpm}.
The resulting output is a vector, so applying a scalar attribution method requires choosing which aspect of the action prediction to explain.
This choice also affects how the explanation is evaluated.

Integrated Gradients (IG)~\citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic} integrates the gradients of a scalar output along a path from a baseline to an input.
For a fixed-noise action sample, this scalar can measure the discrepancy from a detached reference action.
A quadratic discrepancy and a negative norm both measure change, but they define different scalar functions.
Evaluating the attribution requires a second choice.
The attribution ranking selects which features to remove or insert, and a response scores those interventions.
If a comparison changes both the scalar target and the response, its native-response AUC alone cannot identify an improvement in the ranking.

We study this problem using exact derivations, constructive examples and a retrospective RDT case study.
Changing the response from a quadratic discrepancy to a stabilized negative $\ell_2$ discrepancy changes normalized AUC even when the selected features and perturbed outputs stay fixed.
At the same time, the positive factor relating the targets' gradients varies along the path, which allows their IG rankings to differ.
The first operation rescores existing interventions, while the second recomputes the attributions.
Neither result establishes which ranking is more useful under a common response.

A separate question is whether the computed attribution accurately approximates the specified mathematical quantity.
Small scalar completeness residuals can hide errors in individual coordinates.
Repeating the same finite-precision calculation can also reproduce the same inaccurate approximation.
The smooth construction in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aliasing} shows that several nested grids can agree exactly in completeness, ranking and perturbation responses while reversing the exact IG ranking.
We therefore report the numerical diagnostics already collected together with the full planned roster and its unfinished cells.
Successful checks from part of this roster do not certify the implementation.
The intended attribution measures action discrepancy at a fixed context.
It does not explain eventual task success or establish safe robot behavior.

Prior work already shows that random-order AUC depends on interactions and that an attribution ordering need not optimize a perturbation score~\citep{hama2023deletion}.
We examine these concerns for quadratic and stabilized negative-norm action discrepancies.
We make three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Response and path-weight analysis.} We derive an exact normalized-response identity and state its overshoot and zero-gap qualifications. We also construct a finite-stabilizer example in which the quadratic and negative-norm targets reverse IG feature order. A 100-feature equal-contribution policy provides a calibration example for these responses on the historical evaluation grid.
\item \textbf{An authenticated retrospective case study.} We rescore preserved RDT perturbation curves while keeping each recorded ranking fixed. The analysis records the occurrence rules, episode grouping and links from results to source records. Unknown historical context identities prevent a paired comparison of ranking quality.
\item \textbf{Numerical diagnostic limits.} We give a smooth counterexample to certification by finite-grid agreement and report fixed-input repeatability and integration-budget checks from a partially completed campaign. Failures and incomplete coverage remain visible, and no production setting is approved.
\end{enumerate}
These contributions separate the issues and document their consequences in one diffusion-policy family.
A common-response ranking study would answer the additional question of ranking efficacy.
Its absence limits that conclusion without invalidating the analysis that holds rankings fixed.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Attribution Methods}

\citet{sundararajan2017axiomatic} introduced IG with the sensitivity and implementation-invariance axioms and showed that straight-line path integration satisfies both.
Later studies address limitations of the original method.
\citet{jha2021smoother} computed attributions over neural stochastic differential equation models to produce smoother attribution maps.
Their later work showed that shaping the injected noise further improves robustness~\citep{jha2022shaping}.
\citet{walker2024idg} found that gradients from saturation regions contribute noise and proposed Integrated Decision Gradients, which concentrates integration on the decision-relevant portion of the path.
For vision transformers, metric-driven attributions~\citep{walker2025metric} and information-flow decompositions~\citep{walker2025infoflow} improve patch-level explanations.
Guided IG~\citep{kapishnikov2021guided} changes the integration path to reduce pixel-level noise.

Other work directly examines sensitivity to the integration budget.
\citet{makino2024integration} study completeness residuals that vary across instances, nonmonotone changes with step count and unresolved path gradients.
\citet{singhi2024strengthening} examine step-count sensitivity in IG variants and the Important Direction Gradient Integration (IDGI) framework.
Their reported stability experiment compares JPEG-compressed and uncompressed inputs.
Our diagnostics hold the context fixed and examine repeatability separately from coordinate changes across budgets.
The smooth construction in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aliasing} shows a limit of these checks.
Agreement on finitely many grids and exact scalar completeness do not establish accurate coordinate integrals.

\citet{petsiuk2018rise} introduced the insertion and deletion curves that we use as ranking-based faithfulness metrics.
Several studies examine the reliability of perturbation metrics.
\citet{hooker2019roar} evaluate attributions by retraining on ablated inputs.
\citet{rong2022road} show that deletion without retraining leaks information through the shape of the mask itself.
\citet{tomsett2020sanity} also find that common saliency metrics are unreliable.
This literature already establishes that evaluation depends on the metric.
Our case study examines that dependence for action-discrepancy responses by relating their normalized curves algebraically and rescoring preserved interventions while holding the feature ordering fixed.

\citet{hama2023deletion} analyze insertion and deletion scores through an anchored decomposition of the response.
Their Section~3.3 derives random-order expectations and shows that interactions can move the expected curve area away from its straight-line reference.
Their Section~3.4 also gives a case in which Shapley ordering does not maximize AUC.
Appendix~B.2 gives sufficient conditions for optimal ordering under increasing transformations of additive scores on the intervention vertices.
Its IG argument uses a linear score along the continuous path, whose gradients remain proportional to a fixed coefficient vector.
Our nonlinear construction allows the coordinate gradient proportions to vary along that path.
We examine the specific quadratic and stabilized negative-norm responses, their finite-stabilizer rank reversal and their overshoot behavior in saved RDT interventions.
The finite-grid calibration example illustrates the established random-reference concern in this setting.

\citet{adebayo2018sanity} proposed model and data randomization tests to assess whether saliency maps depend on learned parameters and training data.
Their results show why visually plausible maps do not establish this dependence.
Our backbone randomizations are limited because the learned encoders and adaptors remain in place.
For diffusion generative models in the image domain, cross-attention maps relate generated pixels to prompt tokens~\citep{tang2023daam}.
This differs from attributing the scalar discrepancy of a predicted control chunk.

Our implementation applies IG to a fixed-noise action-discrepancy function with modality-specific baselines.
Path weighting is already used in attribution methods such as IDG~\citep{walker2024idg}.
The factor derived here follows specifically from changing the scalar discrepancy target.
We apply these established concerns to quadratic and stabilized negative-norm action discrepancies and examine their separate effects on fixed interventions and integrated rankings.

\subsection{Vision-Language-Action Models}

VLA models combine perception, language understanding and action generation.
RT-2~\citep{zitkovich2023rt2} fine-tunes a vision-language model to produce discretized actions as token sequences.
Octo~\citep{team2024octo} trains a generalist policy on a large multi-robot dataset using a transformer backbone.
Diffusion Policy~\citep{chi2023diffusion} treats action generation as conditional denoising diffusion~\citep{ho2020ddpm}.
It produces action chunks through iterative refinement rather than single-step regression.
RDT~\citep{liu2024rdt} scales this approach using a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone, a SigLIP vision encoder~\citep{zhai2023siglip}, and a T5-XXL language encoder~\citep{raffel2020t5}.
RDT processes six camera slots, consisting of three camera views over a two-frame history, through SigLIP to produce 4{,}374 vision tokens.
It combines these with up to 1{,}024 language tokens and a 128-dimensional unified state vector.
It then generates 64-step action chunks through a five-step DPM-Solver++~\citep{lu2022dpm} denoising schedule.
The combination of modalities and repeated denoising steps makes RDT an interesting but challenging model for attribution.

\subsection{Interpretability in Robotics}

Earlier work on explaining robotic policies focused on reinforcement learning agents in discrete-action settings.
\citet{greydanus2018visualizing} applied perturbation-based saliency to Atari agents.
Counterfactual methods~\citep{amitai2024explaining} explain policies by comparing the outcomes of executed and alternative actions.
In continuous control, VisualBackProp~\citep{bojarski2018visualbackprop} visualizes convolutional neural network (CNN) saliency for end-to-end driving policies.
These are convolutional driving models rather than multimodal diffusion samplers.
Recent VLA studies examine different quantities to explain.
\citet{zhang2026embodied} estimate visual influence using randomized perturbations and action-discrepancy responses, with explicit assumptions that relate mean-squared error to distributional divergence.
VLA-Trace~\citep{shi2026vlatrace} combines representation comparisons, attention interventions and behavioral probes.
IMPACT-VLA~\citep{kim2026impact} attributes phase-modality interventions to downstream task success through counterfactual closed-loop execution.
Our estimand is the discrepancy of a fixed-noise predicted action chunk at a fixed context.
It does not explain eventual task success.
We examine how response geometry affects the evaluation of IG rankings for this estimand and do not claim to originate VLA interpretability.

\section{Attribution Targets and Evaluation}

\subsection{How Integrated Gradients Is Computed}

We start with a differentiable function $F \!:\! \mathbb{R}^n \!\to\! \mathbb{R}$, an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and a baseline $x' \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
Integrated Gradients measures each input dimension's contribution by following the straight path from the baseline to the input.
For dimension $i$, this gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}_i(x, x') = (x_i - x'_i) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\bigg|_{x' + \alpha(x - x')} d\alpha .
\label{eq:ig}
\end{equation}
IG satisfies \emph{completeness} when the fundamental theorem of calculus and the chain rule apply along this path.
This means that $\sum_i \mathrm{IG}_i(x, x') = F(x) - F(x')$.
We approximate the integral using a uniform Riemann sum with $m$ steps, which gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}_i \approx \frac{x_i - x'_i}{m+1} \sum_{r=0}^{m} \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\bigg|_{x' + \frac{r}{m}(x - x')} .
\label{eq:riemann}
\end{equation}
The historical implementation gives equal weight to every sampled point, including both endpoints.
Convergence depends on the regularity of the function being integrated along the path.
At the same time, adding values in finite precision creates another source of error.
Increasing $m$ does not separate this error from integration error and does not necessarily reduce it.

\subsection{Keeping the Target Fixed Through Denoising}
\label{sec:target}

At policy call $t$, we write the sampler output as $g_\theta(o_t,z_t)$, where $o_t$ is the observation and $z_t$ is the fixed diffusion noise.
We call this predicted action chunk $\mu_t$ to keep the notation consistent with the historical records.
It is a sample produced with fixed noise.
The notation does not establish that it is a conditional mean.
We compute the reference chunk $a_t=g_\theta(o_t,z_t)$ once without gradients.
For an input changed by an intervention, the main attribution target is the auxiliary score
\begin{equation}
Q_t=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2D}\lVert a_t-\mu_t\rVert^2,
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^2=1$ and $D=512$ active entries cover 64 predicted time steps over eight active dimensions.
This score is the part of a Gaussian log kernel that changes with the action difference, averaged over entries.
The log-normalizing constant is left out.
It has not been derived as the diffusion policy's conditional log likelihood.
The historical code and files call this target \texttt{logpi}.
We use $\Delta Q$ for its change.
Attribution covers the complete predicted chunk, while the execution loop applies a subsampled prefix before replanning or stopping.

We use the same noise realization at every point on the interpolation path.
This keeps the scalar function deterministic throughout the calculation.
Explaining an expectation over noise would measure a different quantity.
It would require justification for interchanging the gradient and expectation, along with a Monte Carlo treatment.
Drawing independent noise at every path point does not compute the fixed-noise quantity studied here.
The historical implementation replaced nonfinite gradient entries with zero.
This can change individual coordinates even when their errors cancel in the sum.
As a result, the scalar completeness residual can be small or even zero while the coordinates remain wrong.
Completeness alone cannot establish coordinate accuracy.

\subsection{Other Scalar Targets}
\label{sec:targets}

We compare the quadratic target with two other ways to measure the action difference,
\begin{equation}
y_t^{\ell_2}=-\sqrt{\lVert a_t-\mu_t\rVert_2^2+\epsilon},
\qquad y_t^{\mathrm{max}}=-\max_j|a_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j}|,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon=10^{-12}$ is the stabilizer used in the implementation.
Both targets use the same $D$ active entries.
The stabilized $\ell_2$ target is smooth in the residual and has zero gradient when the actions agree exactly.
Away from the stabilizer scale, its gradient in action space approaches unit norm.
The maximum-deviation target, called maxdev, uses a different norm.
It is not generally a monotone transform of $\ell_2$.
For example, the residuals $(1,1)$ and $(1.1,0)$ reverse order under these norms.
For a fixed perturbation, the quadratic and stabilized $\ell_2$ responses have an algebraic relationship.
However, their gradients can still produce different attribution rankings.
Their units and scales also differ, so a larger magnitude alone does not show that a target provides more information or is more useful.
Section~\ref{sec:targets-results} separates the response changes for the quadratic and stabilized $\ell_2$ targets.
The maximum-deviation records remain an additional diagnostic in the released analysis.

\subsection{How the Response Changes the Weight Along the Path}
\label{sec:path-weights}

We hold the reference action, active coordinates, model, noise and other modalities fixed.
We then write $r(x)=\lVert a-\mu(x)\rVert_2^2$ and $L(x)=-\sqrt{r(x)+\epsilon}$.
Where the gradients exist, the chain rule gives the exact relationship
\begin{equation}
\nabla Q(x)=\frac{\sqrt{r(x)+\epsilon}}{D\sigma^2}\nabla L(x).
\label{eq:gradient-weight}
\end{equation}
This means that the nonzero gradients point in the same direction at each point.
Both gradients are zero when the prediction exactly matches its own reference.
Along the path $x(\alpha)=x'+\alpha(x-x')$, the integrated gradient becomes
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}^{Q}_i=(x_i-x_i')\int_0^1
\frac{\sqrt{r(x(\alpha))+\epsilon}}{D\sigma^2}
\partial_i L(x(\alpha))\,d\alpha.
\label{eq:weighted-ig}
\end{equation}
The positive weight generally changes with $\alpha$.
Different coordinates can contribute at different parts of the path.
Changing the weight on those parts can therefore change the final ranking after integration.
This is consistent with IG implementation invariance, which compares different implementations of the same scalar function~\citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}.

For a concrete example, take the scalar action $\mu(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+\tfrac45x_2$, baseline $(0,0)$, actual input $(1,1)$, detached reference $a=\tfrac95$, and $D=\sigma^2=1$.
Exact integration along the same straight path gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}^{Q}=\left(\frac{23}{30},\frac{64}{75}\right),
\qquad
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}\mathrm{IG}^{L}=\left(1,\frac45\right).
\label{eq:rank-reversal}
\end{equation}
Quadratic IG puts feature 2 first.
The stabilized $L$ target with the implemented $\epsilon=10^{-12}$ puts feature 1 first, with a margin of at least $0.1999975$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:geometry-proofs} proves this bound with the finite stabilizer.
The reversal therefore does not depend on replacing the implemented response with its limiting value.
This example shows that a reversal can occur in a smooth nonlinear policy.
It does not show that rankings always change or that this effect occurs empirically in RDT.

\subsection{Attribution at Each Policy Call}

RDT outputs chunks of 64 action steps, which are subsampled $4\times$ to 16 environment steps.
We run IG once per \emph{policy call} because the chunk is produced as one decision.
We therefore attribute one policy query at a time rather than each environment step.
Runtime depends on the implementation, hardware, precision and integration budget.
The earlier timing summaries are not used as a performance result.

At each policy call, we compute attribution separately for each modality using its own forward function.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vision.} We keep language and state fixed and interpolate the adapted image tokens from the baseline to the real observation.
\item \textbf{Language.} We keep vision and state fixed and interpolate the adapted language tokens from a minimal two-token baseline, encoded through T5-XXL, to the real instruction embedding.
\item \textbf{State.} We keep vision and language fixed and interpolate the 128-dimensional unified state vector from zeros to its real value. For the Franka arm, eight dimensions are active. These represent seven joints and the gripper. The state-adaptor multilayer perceptron (MLP) stays inside the forward function so that the calculation preserves the structure of each joint.
\end{itemize}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:perstep} shows this procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{IG at each policy call with fixed noise. Section~\ref{sec:setup} explains which historical artifacts are available and their limits.}
\label{alg:perstep}
\textbf{Input.} Policy $\pi_\theta$, environment $\mathcal{E}$, integration steps $m$, target $y \in \{Q, \ell_2, \mathrm{maxdev}\}$\\
\textbf{Output.} Attributions $\{A_t^V, A_t^L, A_t^S\}$ at every policy call.
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $o_0 \leftarrow \mathcal{E}.\text{reset}()$
\FOR{policy call $t = 0, 1, \ldots$}
    \STATE Encode $o_t$ into vision $v_t$, language $l_t$, state $s_t$
    \STATE Draw and store $z_t$. Compute $a_t \leftarrow g_\theta(o_t,z_t)$ without gradients
    \STATE Define $F$ from $a_t$, the fixed-noise predicted chunk $\mu_t$, and the chosen target
    \FOR{modality $M \in \{V, L, S\}$}
        \STATE Set baseline $x'_M$, input $x_M$
        \STATE Freeze the other two modalities
        \STATE $A_t^M \leftarrow \text{IG}(F_M, x_M, x'_M, m)$ \hfill Eq.~\ref{eq:riemann}
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Record $A_t^V, A_t^L, A_t^S$ and completeness errors
    \STATE Apply action chunk and advance $\mathcal{E}$ by up to 16 steps, stopping on termination
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Choosing the baseline.}
The vision baseline is a uniform gray image in the external-camera slot.
The other five camera slots contain constant background images and remain fixed at their real values.
This means that IG attributes only to the slot that contains information about the scene.
The language baseline is a minimal two-token sequence made from the tokenizer's padding and end-of-sequence tokens.
T5 has no beginning-of-sequence token.
We encode this sequence through T5-XXL instead of using a zero vector.
We then add zeros to the encoder output until it reaches the stored embedding length.
The language forward passes attend over the real token span for the task.
As a result, the attended baseline contains the encoded token pair followed by zero vectors at the remaining attended positions.
The GELU-based language adaptor can accept a zero vector numerically, but that would define a different representation baseline.
The preliminary comparisons of effectiveness cannot be independently reconstructed.
The state baseline is a zero vector in normalized joint space.
This choice does not establish that it represents a physically supported state or a useful reference for an explanation.
Appendix~\ref{sec:anchoring} reports the available checks of sensitivity to the vision baseline.

\subsection{How We Evaluate the Attributions}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Completeness.}
For each modality, we keep the other inputs fixed and report the absolute residual
$|\sum_i\mathrm{IG}_i-(F(x)-F(x'))|$ and its relative version
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Err}=\frac{|\sum_i\mathrm{IG}_i-(F(x)-F(x'))|}{|F(x)-F(x')|}.
\end{equation}
The relative quantity is undefined when the signed gap is exactly zero.
A small nonzero gap can make numerical error appear large after division.
The historical action-norm filter $\|a_t\|\geq15$ does not place a bound on this denominator.
We retain it only to check how changing the included population affects the results.
A low residual shows agreement in a scalar accounting identity.
Errors in individual coordinates can still cancel in the sum while changing the attribution map or ranking.

\paragraph{Perturbation curves.}
We rank eligible feature groups by adding the absolute coordinate attributions within each group.
For vision, the groups are patch tokens from the external camera.
For language, they are the attended instruction tokens.
Deletion replaces the first groups in the ranking with their baseline features.
Insertion starts from the baseline input and replaces the first groups with their original features.
For a scalar response $R$, the normalized value at each point is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde R(x_k)=\frac{R(x_k)-R(x')}{R(x)-R(x')}.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
We integrate using the fraction grid stored in each record.
The curve and its AUC can both fall outside $[0,1]$.
The historical grid requests $\{0,1,5,10,20,30,50,75,100\}\%$ of the groups.
Rounding changes the actual fractions of language tokens.
The saved curves therefore cannot be treated as continuous functions sampled at uniform intervals.
Top-$k$ score changes are $R(x_k)-R(x)$, with units determined by $R$.
We keep the attribution target that produces the ranking separate from the response that scores its interventions.
The revised tables do not use the earlier study-specific thresholds as tests of effectiveness.

\paragraph{Randomization and baseline checks.}
C1 reinitializes model parameters and measures the correlation between the resulting attributions and the originals.
The standard version changes the final action-output projection and recomputes its reference action.
The frozen-reference and backbone-cascade versions keep the original reference.
The historical cascade reinitializes all backbone parameter matrices of rank at least two at the same time.
It keeps one-dimensional parameters, encoders and adaptors.
It therefore does not progressively randomize one layer at a time.
C2 shuffles both the external-camera pixels and the language.
The historical implementation uses a pre-T5 shuffled-language cache when one is available.
Otherwise, it permutes the post-T5 embeddings.
The saved records do not establish which mode was used for each call.
These tests measure different forms of dependence.
C2 is not the label-randomization experiment of \citet{adebayo2018sanity}.
Historical perturbed maps use $m=16$, while original maps nominally use $m=64$.
C1 therefore changes the numerical approximation as well as the model weights.
We also compare black, gray and blurred observation features to check baseline sensitivity.

\section{Retrospective Data and Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Scope and traceability.}
The revised analysis checks the bytes of \ArchiveFiles{} preserved files containing \ArchiveValidRecords{} valid records and \ArchiveQuarantined{} specifically identified corrupt line.
Each valid physical record is identified by its source path, original line number and byte hash.
Some recorded keys occur more than once. The inventory contains \ArchiveDuplicateGroups{} such groups, and \ArchiveConflictingGroups{} of them differ in scientific fields as well as, or instead of, timing metadata.
We use the last occurrence as a declared rule for this retrospective analysis. This rule does not establish that the selected attempt is correct when the records conflict.
To show how those conflicts affect the results, a separate sensitivity analysis removes every key with a conflicting payload.
The released lineage registry links the records and occurrence decisions to population membership, result identifiers and generated manuscript cells.
These links allow the preserved bytes to be checked. They cannot recover missing checkpoints, observations, action tensors or noise.

\paragraph{Campaigns and models.}
The main reconstructed RDT-170M tables use the saved Month 7 verification campaign at $m=64$. That campaign covers PickCube, StackCube, PegInsertionSide and PickSingleYCB in ManiSkill3~\citep{tao2024maniskill3}.
These experiments are separate from the original main pass, whose inputs are unavailable.
The verification campaign used pretrained RDT-170M, while the saved RDT-1B PickCube campaign used a task-finetuned checkpoint.
The comparison therefore changes training, trajectories and historical preprocessing along with parameter count. It cannot isolate the effect of model scale or competence.
For this reason, Appendix~\ref{sec:secondary-historical} reports the 1B cohort separately.
The primary tables do not include missing original completeness or standard-sanity results, or missing early PickCube/StackCube integration-budget results.

\paragraph{Known protocol limits.}
The historical attribution covers the full predicted action chunk, although the simulator executes a subsampled chunk.
The choice of four tasks also reflects historical implementation decisions. An attempted TurnFaucet run used an incorrect controller, and no corrected rerun is documented. Its unsuccessful outcomes cannot establish that the model lacked the capability for that task.
Early follow-up jobs replaced encoded padding/end tokens with a zero language baseline. The PickSingleYCB follow-up also used a proxy PickCube instruction embedding.
As a result, the pooled 1B language cohort contains both the early baseline and later encoded baselines.
Early sidecar filenames omitted the seed and integration budget. This allowed files to be overwritten and possibly reused across nominal runs.
The seed-tagged Month 7 records reduce that filename problem, but they do not recover the old contexts.
Matching scalar fingerprints alone therefore do not establish exact context equality.

\paragraph{Estimands and uncertainty.}
The primary population contains all valid saved calls after applying the declared occurrence rule.
We summarize continuous call-level quantities by their medians over calls. Residual pass rates are proportions over the stated call population. Solver contrasts report relative changes between endpoint medians, while the separately named equal-episode analysis uses episode medians.
For uncertainty, we resample whole recorded episode groups, identified by task, model, recorded seed and episode, with $B=10{,}000$ draws and seed zero.
Each point estimate and its interval use exactly the same source-record population.
The resulting percentile intervals describe the released empirical mixture of episodes. Longer episodes contribute more calls to that mixture, and the intervals do not measure variability across independently trained checkpoints.
A separately named 1B sensitivity gives each episode equal weight by reporting the median of episode medians.
For the old randomization variants, we also group nominal task/episode keys across seeds when they may have been reused. This is a conditional sensitivity to dependence between those records.
Neither grouping can recover unobserved context identity.
We report very small cluster counts explicitly. In particular, resampling two baseline groups is not evidence of calibrated interval coverage.
All unfiltered results and the historical norm-selected sensitivity remain available. We do not remove a tail to obtain a favorable threshold verdict.

The additional paired rescoring analysis holds each call's ranking and interventions fixed. It defines $\Delta_{ej}=\operatorname{AUC}_{Q,ej}-\operatorname{AUC}_{L,ej}$ within call $j$ of recorded episode $e$.
Its primary estimand is $E^{-1}\sum_{e=1}^E n_e^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_e}\Delta_{ej}$, which gives episodes equal weight. The median of paired call differences is a prespecified descriptive sensitivity.
Both summarize changes within calls, so neither is the difference between the marginal medians reported above.
Before computing these summaries, the protocol specified all eight ranking/modality/direction combinations and the same $10{,}000$-draw episode-bootstrap convention.
The intervals are conditional descriptions without a multiplicity adjustment. We do not claim calibrated joint coverage or global significance.
If either response has a zero endpoint gap, the paired difference remains undefined and stays in the accounting. All 750 calls in each ranking cohort have defined paired differences. Each of the 30 recorded episodes contains 25 calls, so the equal-episode and equal-call means coincide here.

\section{Response Geometry and Retrospective Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Separating Attribution Target from Evaluation Response}
\label{sec:targets-results}

We can change the response used to score a recorded intervention without recomputing IG or changing any selected feature.
Let $d$ be the recorded active-action discrepancy, $Q=-d^2/1024$, and $L=-\sqrt{d^2+\epsilon}$ with $\epsilon=10^{-12}$.
The responses are related by
\begin{equation}
L=-\sqrt{-1024Q+\epsilon},\qquad Q=-\frac{L^2-\epsilon}{1024}.
\label{eq:rescore}
\end{equation}
When the actual endpoint is an exact self-reference and the all-baseline squared residual is $R>0$, their normalized values satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
N_Q(r)&=1-\frac rR,\qquad
N_L(r)=\frac{\sqrt{R+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{R+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon},\\
N_Q(r)-N_L(r)&=\frac{(\sqrt{r+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon)(\sqrt{R+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon})}{R}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:normalized-geometry}
\end{equation}
Thus $N_Q>N_L$ for $0<r<R$. The responses are equal at both endpoints, and their order reverses for $r>R$.
If every intervention stays in range, integration with nonnegative weights preserves this weak ordering even when the curve is not monotone. The area inequality is strict only if an interior residual receives positive integration weight.
Overshoots can reverse the area ordering, so they must be retained rather than clipped. If $R=0$, neither normalization is defined.
For an in-range curve, changing from $Q$ to $L$ therefore lowers both insertion and deletion AUC.
Larger insertion AUC and smaller deletion AUC are often interpreted as better ranking quality. Under that interpretation, this response change points to opposite changes in quality even though the interventions stay the same.
The historical computation does not assume that the recorded actual endpoint is an exact self-reference.
Instead, we transform every saved curve ordinate, compute its endpoints under the transformed response, and integrate again on the same saved grid.
After duplicate handling, each ranking cohort contains \RescoreCalls{} calls from \RescoreEpisodes{} episodes.
For quadratic-target vision rankings, the median deletion AUC changes from \RescoreQDeletionQ{} under $Q$ to \RescoreQDeletionL{} under $L$.
For stabilized-$\ell_2$ rankings, it changes from \RescoreLDeletionL{} under $L$ to \RescoreLDeletionQ{} under $Q$.
Table~\ref{tab:targets} and Figure~\ref{fig:response-geometry} show both modalities and both perturbation directions.

\subsection{An Analytic Random-Order Calibration Example}
\label{sec:random-null}

Random-order AUC need not match a straight-line reference when the scored response contains interactions~\citep{hama2023deletion}.
The following example gives the exact areas for our quadratic response and finite evaluation grids.
Consider the finite additive scalar policy $f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$, with an all-one input, a zero baseline and the self-reference $f(x)=n$.
Every feature ordering produces the same perturbation values.
Interventions on whole-feature prefixes realize only the fractions $q=k/n$, with $k=0,\ldots,n$. At those fractions, the normalized quadratic deletion and insertion values are $1-q^2$ and $1-(1-q)^2$.
Trapezoidal integration over all $n+1$ prefix fractions gives
\[
\operatorname{AUC}_{n}=\frac23-\frac{1}{6n^2}
\]
for either curve.
Integrating the polynomial extensions over continuous $q\in[0,1]$ instead gives $2/3$. This is also the limit as $n\to\infty$, but it is not the exact finite-grid area.
With $n=100$, every fraction on the historical nine-point requested grid is realized exactly. Trapezoidal integration on that grid gives \RandomGridAUC{} for both curves and every ordering.
The policy is additive, but its quadratic discrepancy response contains interactions between features.
This finite example illustrates why a universal random-order AUC of 0.5 does not apply to that response.
For comparison, an unstabilized negative-norm response is linear on the same fractions and has trapezoidal AUC 0.5.
A fixed AUC reference such as 0.5 must therefore be justified for the particular policy, response and intervention protocol.
This construction is an analytic control. It is not an empirical measurement of random-ranking performance on RDT.

\section{Numerical Diagnostics from an Incomplete Campaign}
\label{sec:numerical-validation}

The later diagnostic campaign tests identified current computations, separately from the historical records above.
Historical rescoring applies arithmetic to saved curves whose original contexts cannot be recovered. The later probe instead evaluates a separately identified offline fp32 function on newly authenticated contexts, using its own reference actions.
It cannot validate the historical attribution maps or provide a paired comparison with them.
The campaign stopped because of compute cost before its planned numerical coverage and independent verification were complete.
We analyze a fixed, authenticated snapshot of sealed results. Entries that were unfinished at that point are not classified as successful or failed calculations.
The stopping decision gives no statistical sampling rule for the completed subset. Counts of passed or violated criteria therefore describe that subset, rather than estimate population failure rates.

\paragraph{Functions and precision controls.}
Stored observations, masks, reference actions and exact noise arrays allow comparisons on fixed, authenticated inputs.
The diagnostic implementation separates accumulation arithmetic, master-path and final-product arithmetic, quadrature, and downstream model precision.
It compares full coordinate arrays, ranks over eligible groups, selected groups and perturbation responses.
An earlier downstream-fp32 factor control lifts the same bf16-rounded parameters and cached adapted representations. It also explicitly converts non-parameter dtype attributes and keeps the original reference action fixed.
This changes downstream arithmetic while keeping the numeric inputs fixed. It does not recover unrounded checkpoint parameters or test fp32 encoders.
Keeping the original reference also makes this control different from the later probe.
The earlier comparisons came before the full repeated-gradient checks, so they cannot alone establish a globally repeatable precision contrast.

The later engineering campaign defines a separate deterministic fp32 function. Image and language representations keep their cached bf16 values lifted to fp32, raw state is re-adapted in fp32, and the function generates its own fp32 action reference.
It uses contexts visited by the bf16 policy and the stored source noise.
This is an offline intervention function. It is neither the bf16 behavior policy nor a derivative of that policy's executed commands.
Repeating its endpoints checks a different property from repeating its path gradients and attribution maps.
Changing the function or its reference therefore cannot be interpreted as changing quadrature alone.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The saved records let us separate three questions: what a scalar response measures, whether a numerical attribution is reliable, and whether its ranking contains useful information that depends on the model.
Rescoring fixed recorded interventions answers the first question because it changes the response while keeping the selected features fixed.
This operation alone cannot answer the other two questions.
Our claims therefore concern exact relationships and measurements reconstructed from the records, with numerical diagnostics limited to their stated coverage.
Establishing ranking efficacy would require a new paired study with common responses and informative controls. The present results do not establish it.
The elementary identities identify which operation changes a reported evaluation. This helps prevent a response change from being interpreted as an empirical improvement in ranking, without proposing a new general theory of attribution.
The random-reference and attribution-versus-AUC distinctions have direct precedent in \citet{hama2023deletion}.
Our contribution concerns the specified action-discrepancy responses, constructive examples and retrospective measurements within the stated limits.
A common response defines the quantity being compared. It does not provide a universal measure of explanation quality.
Its action coordinates, units, baselines, perturbation groups and aggregation need to be justified for the intended use.
Matched random, input-difference and path-gradient controls would help address the unresolved ranking question by separating information in the attribution from simple input structure and the IG multiplier.
The deferred protocols provide no comparative or learned-weight-specific result in this paper.

\paragraph{A practical evaluation procedure.}
These distinctions suggest four steps when reporting action-discrepancy attributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Specify the scalar attribution target and evaluation response separately. Also state the reference action, fixed noise, feature representation, baseline, intervention groups and schedule.
\item Compare rankings on authenticated matched contexts under each common response. Claims of efficacy need matched random and simple ranking controls. To examine a response change, rescore the same interventions, recompute the endpoint normalization and retain overshoots and undefined gaps.
\item Check repeatability and sensitivity of coordinates, rankings and responses across numerical settings on a declared context roster. Retain failures and incomplete cases. Completeness and agreement on finitely many grids help diagnose numerical behavior, but they do not certify exact IG.
\item Report the unit of analysis and dependence grouping. Distinguish response sensitivity from numerical reliability and ranking quality, because none of these alone establishes improved closed-loop behavior.
\end{enumerate}
This procedure clarifies what each comparison identifies. It does not determine a universally adequate integration budget or evaluation response.

The historical records also contain several sources of uncertainty that require different treatment.
Source-record hashes can authenticate the released bytes, but checkpoint and context identities can remain unknown.
Episode resampling addresses dependence within episodes conditional on a particular empirical mixture. It cannot repair shared or overwritten contexts, preprocessing confounds or missing training replicates.
The 1B results come from one checkpoint. The solver and alternative-target analyses also cover narrower task populations than the verification campaign.
Endpoint normalization can produce unbounded response curves, making the results sensitive to endpoint gaps.
Camera-token and language-token interventions differ in their group counts and meaning. Their AUCs therefore cannot be used interchangeably as measures of modality importance.

We preserve historical overlay panels as artifacts, but they do not establish successful grounding or diagnose behavioral failure.
Groups previously named from completeness residuals likewise describe numerical diagnostics. They are not independently validated labels for outcomes such as misgrounding or delayed credit.
The original main-pass and early budget-extension numbers could not be recovered, so the revised primary tables exclude them.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-budget} reports only later budget cohorts that can be reconstructed. Those cohorts do not establish convergence.

\section{Conclusion}

An explicit auxiliary action-discrepancy score defines per-policy-call IG for a fixed-noise diffusion sampler.
For the saved RDT perturbation curves, changing only the evaluation response changes normalized AUC while leaving the selected features fixed.
All eight reported paired cases have positive median changes. At the same time, overshooting tails make six means negative, which shows why the summary statistic and endpoint geometry both affect the conclusion.
A finite-grid calibration example illustrates the response dependence of the random-ranking reference for the same evaluation protocol.
The canonical retrospective tables retain the measurements supported by the available records and exclude primary constants that could not be recovered.
The actual-model diagnostics preserve unresolved numerical failures and incomplete coverage.
The smooth aliasing construction shows a further limit: finite grids can agree while producing an ordering different from exact IG.
A common response is needed to interpret a comparison of ranking quality. Our results do not establish that one target or attribution method performs better under that response.

\section{Response-Geometry Derivations}
\label{sec:geometry-proofs}

The identities in Section~\ref{sec:path-weights} hold the action reference fixed and assume that the relevant path gradients are integrable.
Differentiating $Q=-r/(2D\sigma^2)$ and $L=-\sqrt{r+\epsilon}$ gives Equation~\ref{eq:gradient-weight} directly.
For Equation~\ref{eq:normalized-geometry}, set $a_0=\sqrt\epsilon$, $b=\sqrt{R+\epsilon}$ and $z=\sqrt{r+\epsilon}$.
Because $R=b^2-a_0^2$ and $r=z^2-a_0^2$, rationalization gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{b^2-z^2}{R}-\frac{b-z}{b-a_0}
=\frac{(z-a_0)(b-z)}{R}.
\end{equation}
For in-range residuals, the maximum difference is $(b-a_0)^2/(4R)\le1/4$.
These pointwise statements apply to interventions from a fixed ranking. They do not order different rankings or establish improvements in task success.

For the rank-reversal example, let $c=4/5$ and parameterize the path by $(t,t)$.
The scalar residual is $d(t)=1+c-t^2-ct=(1-t)(1+t+c)\ge0$.
The quadratic path integrands are $2td(t)$ and $cd(t)$, which give
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}^{Q}=\left(\frac12+\frac c3,\frac{2c}3+\frac{c^2}2\right)
=\left(\frac{23}{30},\frac{64}{75}\right).
\end{equation}
For the stabilized negative norm, the corresponding integrands are $2t\,d(t)/\sqrt{d(t)^2+\epsilon}$ and $c\,d(t)/\sqrt{d(t)^2+\epsilon}$.
The bound $d(t)\ge c(1-t)$ and monotonicity of $1-d/\sqrt{d^2+\epsilon}$ give
\begin{equation}
\int_0^1\left(1-\frac{d(t)}{\sqrt{d(t)^2+\epsilon}}\right)dt
\le1-\frac{\sqrt{c^2+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon}{c}
\le\frac{\sqrt\epsilon}{c}.
\end{equation}
Therefore $0\le1-\mathrm{IG}^{L}_1\le2\sqrt\epsilon/c=2.5\times10^{-6}$ and $0\le c-\mathrm{IG}^{L}_2\le\sqrt\epsilon=10^{-6}$ at the implemented stabilizer.
All coordinates are positive. The stabilized $L$ margin is at least $0.1999975$, while $Q$ gives the opposite ordering with exact margin $13/150$.
The artifact checks these identities through independent differentiation, integration and normalization. Those checks include overshoots, zero gaps and a policy example without rank reversal.

\label{sec:episode-influence}

Large finite response changes affect the paired summaries in Table~\ref{tab:paired-rescoring}.
To locate this sensitivity, we omit each of the 30 recorded episodes in turn,
retain all calls from the other 29 episodes, and recompute their equally
weighted episode mean. Table~\ref{tab:episode-influence} reports every case.
This analysis followed an exploratory audit of the same records. Its separately
saved protocol is therefore not an outcome-blind preregistration.
The omission ranges describe deterministic influence. They are not confidence
intervals and do not establish that the recorded episodes form an independent sample.
The full untrimmed means, medians and conditional intervals remain unchanged.

All four negative insertion means remain negative after every individual
omission. The Q language-deletion mean changes sign in 3 of 30 omissions,
and the L2 language-deletion mean changes sign in 1 of 30. Both already have
conditional bootstrap intervals spanning zero. In the latter case, the most
negative episode contributes $97.29\%$ of the sum of magnitudes of negative
episode means. This measures concentration among negative episode means,
not a share of the signed net mean. It does not justify excluding that episode.

Endpoint normalization also helps explain the extreme vision-insertion changes.
For the most negative Q-ranking call, the baseline squared residual is
$0.0174195$, the smallest in its 750-call cohort. At the same time, the
maximum saved perturbed residual is $653.053$, giving a ratio of about $37{,}490$.
The finite Q endpoint gap is $1.7011\times10^{-5}$, above the historical
$10^{-9}$ cutoff. The native Q AUC is $-4464.9082$, while its L2 rescore is
$-26.0696$. This call combines a small baseline discrepancy with a large
intervention response. The division is neither undefined nor by a machine-zero
value. These quantities also do not calibrate physical importance in action units.
The artifact provides the same diagnostics within each separate ranking cohort.
Matching nominal episode labels cannot authenticate a cross-arm pair.
