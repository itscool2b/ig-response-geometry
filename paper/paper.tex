\begin{abstract}
Feature attributions rank inputs associated with a model's prediction. Their evaluation depends on how a scalar response scores predictions after features are removed or restored. For robot policies, we separate the attribution target from the scoring response. We compare squared distance (Q) and stabilized distance (N) from a fixed reference action. An identity shows that N cannot increase insertion or deletion area under the curve (AUC) when no intervention moves the prediction farther from the reference, in squared distance, than replacing every feature with its baseline. Strict decreases make an unchanged ranking look worse by insertion and better by deletion; absolute thresholds can give opposite verdicts. We rescore saved curves for PickCube, a simulated pick-and-place task, from a model recorded as a pretrained Robotics Diffusion Transformer. Q-ranked and N-ranked cohorts ranked features using Q and N, respectively; each contains \RescoreCalls{} policy calls from \RescoreEpisodes{} episodes with no successes. Across eight combinations of cohort, modality and direction, median Q-minus-N differences are \PairedMedianMin{} to \PairedMedianMax{}, with \PositiveShareMin{}\% to \PositiveShareMax{}\% positive. Overshoots occur in \OvershootShareMin{}\% to \OvershootShareMax{}\% of calls. Extreme calls, mainly each episode's first prediction, make vision-insertion means strongly negative. An example shows that target choice can also reverse rankings. Separate numerical diagnostics show that integration error can change evaluation results or leave them unchanged. A smooth counterexample shows that agreeing uniform integration grids can give incorrect coordinates. We recommend specifying targets and responses separately, comparing rankings under matched controls, reporting full distributions, and checking numerical sensitivity.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} integrated gradients, attribution evaluation, diffusion policies, perturbation metrics, numerical integration, robotics

\section{Introduction}

Attributions assign image regions, language representations or state coordinates contributions to a robot policy's prediction. A persuasive ranking need not identify inputs that meaningfully affect actions. Diffusion policies predict action sequences by repeatedly refining noise conditioned on observations\citep{chi2023diffusion}. Their vector outputs require a scalar attribution target, such as discrepancy from a fixed reference action\citep{zhang2026embodied}.

Q measures squared distance and N stabilized distance from the reference; ``Q-ranked'' means ranked using target Q. Deletion area under the curve (AUC) summarizes responses as features are removed, with lower conventionally better. Consider removing the highest-ranked image features. In Q-ranked curves, median vision-deletion AUC changes from \RescoreQDeletionQ{} scored with Q to \RescoreQDeletionL{} scored with N. An illustrative, unvalidated absolute bar of 0.40 rejects the first and accepts the second. Absolute thresholds, cross-response comparisons and assumed 0.5 references therefore need response-matched calibration. Only scoring changed; rankings, interventions and predicted actions stayed fixed.

Integrated Gradients (IG) adds model sensitivity along a straight baseline-to-input path\citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}. Completeness means coordinate attributions sum to the target's endpoint change. Three questions differ: what the scoring response measures, whether integration recovers the intended attribution, and whether the ranking is useful. We study the first two using the Robotics Diffusion Transformer (RDT)\citep{liu2024rdt}.

Insertion and deletion scores evaluate a ranking by progressively restoring or removing features\citep{petsiuk2018rise}. Their interpretation depends on the interventions: retraining after removal changes the question\citep{hooker2019roar}, masks can reveal information\citep{rong2022road}, and perturbation metrics can disagree\citep{tomsett2020sanity}. Hama, Mase and Owen established that response scale and feature interactions affect insertion and deletion evaluation and its random-order reference\citep{hama2023deletion}. Here we separate target choice from scoring response for vector-output action discrepancies, derive their response-only ordering, and measure the effect and normalized-AUC instability on saved robot-policy curves.

The integration budget is the number of intervals used to approximate IG. Earlier work finds instance-dependent completeness errors as this budget changes\citep{makino2024integration}; IDGI reduces explanation noise by retaining the component of each integration step along the gradient\citep{yang2023idgi}. Our concern is recovery of the specified IG integral. We use quadrature aliasing, where sampled nodes miss variation between them, to examine what finite numerical checks can establish.

First, scoring with N instead of Q cannot increase either AUC for in-range curves, whose saved residuals all lie between zero and the all-baseline value (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}). Strict decreases make insertion look worse and deletion better; saved curves measure their size and prevalence. Second, overshoots beyond this range can dominate means through extreme first calls. Third, an example shows target-dependent IG ranking reversal. Finally, real-model diagnostics expose numerical problems, while a counterexample limits agreement and completeness checks. Section~\ref{sec:procedure} gives reporting steps.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Research design and policy setting}

We combine algebra, saved-curve rescoring and separate numerical diagnostics. A policy call predicts an action chunk; the first call is index 0 of each episode. Target and scoring response are separate choices (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}); rescoring requires no new policy execution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{figures_revision/pipeline.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Ranking and scoring are separate choices.} Q is the quadratic action-discrepancy function and N its stabilized norm counterpart (Equation~\ref{eq:targets}). IG ranks feature groups using one target. The scoring stage covers both the interventions and their response curves: either response can score the same predicted actions. Rescoring keeps the observation, reference action, diffusion noise, ranking and interventions fixed.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Attribution covers 64 predicted timesteps and eight action dimensions, so $d_a=512$. ``Active'' denotes the robot's eight retained coordinates within the padded 128-dimensional representation, including these coordinates across the action chunk. Our \mbox{ManiSkill3}\citep{tao2024maniskill3} evaluation executes every fourth action: 16 per call and 25 calls give 400 environment steps. The headline task, PickCube, moves a cube to a target. A separate numerical collection covers PickCube (cube placement), \mbox{StackCube} (stacking), \mbox{PegInsertionSide} (peg insertion) and \mbox{PickSingleYCB} (household-object pickup).

\subsection{The function being explained}

Fix context $c$: model, diffusion noise and other modalities. Let $\mathcal{M}$ denote vision, language or state, with input representation $x$ and baseline $x'$. Let $g_{c,\mathcal{M}}(u)$ predict the active action chunk after replacement by $u$, with reference $a_c=g_{c,\mathcal{M}}(x)$ computed once. Define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r(u)&=\|a_c-g_{c,\mathcal{M}}(u)\|_2^2,\\
Q(u)&=-\frac{r(u)}{2d_a},\qquad
N(u)=-\sqrt{r(u)+\epsilon},\qquad \epsilon=10^{-12}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:targets}
\end{equation}
Either function can be the attribution target or scoring response. Both reward proximity to the reference in action coordinates. Q is an auxiliary discrepancy score, not a policy log likelihood. Q's magnitude grows with squared residual, whereas $|N|$ grows approximately with its square root when $r$ is large compared with $\epsilon$. At the actual input,
\[
Q(x)=0,\qquad N(x)=-\sqrt{\epsilon}.
\]
Both gradients vanish there. A positive constant multiplier changes neither exact absolute-coordinate rankings nor endpoint-normalized AUC.

Fixed noise makes the attributed mapping one function along the path. Redrawing noise would change that function. Averaging over noise requires a different target and sampling procedure; interpreting the discrepancy as a distance between action distributions requires further assumptions\citep{zhang2026embodied}.

\subsection{Features, baselines, and Integrated Gradients}

RDT encodes inputs as tokens; a group contains coordinates replaced together. SigLIP image and T5-XXL language encoders feed adaptors mapping outputs into policy representations\citep{liu2024rdt}. Vision attribution follows the adaptor, with 729 patch-token groups from one external camera. Deleted tokens receive adapted tokens at matching positions from a uniform gray image. Five other camera slots remain fixed background images.

Language groups are token positions retained by the attention mask after the encoder and language adaptor. Their count was not preserved in the saved curve records. A deleted position receives its matching adapted baseline token. The baseline encodes padding and end-of-sequence tokens, pads the encoder output with zeros, then applies the adaptor, so its adapted value need not be zero. The encoder is not rerun after replacement; other tokens can retain information from the removed position. Baselines specify a comparison rather than a uniquely neutral input\citep{sturmfels2020visualizing}, and straight paths between language representations need not correspond to valid text\citep{sanyal2021discretized}.

State uses eight active coordinates of the normalized 128-dimensional representation, one per feature group, a zero baseline, and differentiation through its adaptor. Saved PickCube rescoring covers only vision and language; separate numerical diagnostics include state.

For target $F\in\{Q,N\}$, coordinate $i$ has attribution
\begin{equation}
A_i^F=(x_i-x'_i)\int_0^1\partial_i F(x'+\alpha(x-x'))\,d\alpha.
\label{eq:ig}
\end{equation}
Under the chain rule and path integrability, completeness gives $\sum_i A_i^F=F(x)-F(x')$. This total is $r(x')/(2d_a)$ for Q and $\sqrt{r(x')+\epsilon}-\sqrt{\epsilon}$ for N. Vision and language groups are ranked by the sum of absolute coordinate attributions within each group.

With $m$ intervals, $m+1$ evaluations and $f_i(\alpha)=\partial_iF(x'+\alpha(x-x'))$, the saved PickCube runs used the endpoint-inclusive average
\begin{equation}
A_{i,m}^{\mathrm{avg}}=(x_i-x_i')\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{j=0}^{m}f_i(j/m).
\label{eq:endpoint-average}
\end{equation}
The numerical sample uses the composite trapezoidal rule,
\begin{equation}
A_{i,m}^{\mathrm{trap}}=\frac{x_i-x_i'}{m}
\left[\frac{f_i(0)+f_i(1)}2+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}f_i(j/m)\right].
\label{eq:trapezoid}
\end{equation}
The trapezoidal rule is second-order accurate for sufficiently smooth integrands\citep{trefethen2014trapezoidal}. The endpoint-inclusive average equals $m/(m+1)$ times that estimate plus $(x_i-x_i')[f_i(0)+f_i(1)]/[2(m+1)]$, giving a generally first-order endpoint correction. Here ``order'' describes how error decreases as the interval count grows. Positive $\epsilon$ makes N smooth for a smooth action map, but its narrow transition near zero can remain unresolved at practical budgets. The saved runs used $m=64$, brain floating point 16 (bf16), and zero replacement of nonfinite gradients; the replacement frequency was not recorded. The implementation for the saved PickCube runs and the numerical collection specifies five-step DPM-Solver++ sampling, with gradients through every denoising step and noise held fixed within each attribution. The saved PickCube curves lack run configurations, so their step count is inferred from the default configuration.

\subsection{Interventions and scoring}
\label{sec:scoring}

We compare responses on the same interventions. Let $G$ count eligible groups, the camera patches or attended language positions defined above, and $P_k$ mask the first $k$ ranked groups, expanded over their coordinates. With $\odot$ denoting coordinatewise multiplication, insertion and deletion inputs are
\begin{equation}
x_k^{\mathrm{ins}}=x'+P_k\odot(x-x'),\qquad
x_k^{\mathrm{del}}=x-P_k\odot(x-x').
\label{eq:interventions}
\end{equation}
For response $S\in\{Q,N\}$, endpoint normalization gives
\begin{equation}
C_S(u)=\frac{S(u)-S(x')}{S(x)-S(x')}.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
This assigns zero to the all-baseline input and one to the actual input. Self-reference gives $r(x)=0$ by definition; we check it because records were stored in finite precision. Write $B=r(x')>0$ for the all-baseline squared residual. An intervention is in range if $0\le r(u)\le B$ and an overshoot if $r(u)>B$. A curve is fully in range if every saved point is in range. An undershoot below zero is impossible in exact arithmetic; its check guards against roundoff.

Area under the curve (AUC) integrates normalized values over the fraction of groups intervened on. Higher insertion AUC and lower deletion AUC are conventionally better. The saved nominal fractions are 0, 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75 and 100\%. For $0=q_0\le\cdots\le q_J=1$, with $J=8$ fraction intervals and integer group count $k_j$ at fraction $q_j$, we use
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{AUC}_S=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{q_j-q_{j-1}}2
\left[C_S(x_{k_j})+C_S(x_{k_{j-1}})\right].
\label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
Actual fractions $k_j/G$ can differ from the nominal schedule; the realized masks and rounding rule were not preserved with these curves. In all 3,000 language curves, the saved response values at 1\% and 5\% are equal, without establishing mask identity. Both responses use the same nominal AUC weights. Dropping the 1\% node moves language medians by at most \LanguageMedianSensitivity{}; for fully in-range calls, the paired change is bounded by \LanguageInRangeBound{} (Appendix~\ref{sec:response-proof}).

The paired response effect is
\begin{equation}
\Delta=\operatorname{AUC}_Q-\operatorname{AUC}_N.
\label{eq:paired-change}
\end{equation}
Positive $\Delta$ means scoring with N lowers that AUC. Ranking, interventions, predicted actions, baseline and schedule remain fixed within each pair. The median of paired differences need not equal the difference of the two marginal medians.

\subsection{Data sources and claim dependencies}

The saved PickCube runs contain response curves and episode records. One cohort used Q to rank features and the other used N; we call them Q-ranked and N-ranked. Both are recorded as the same pretrained \mbox{RDT-170M} without PickCube fine-tuning; the original checkpoint file was not preserved. \mbox{RDT-170M} is the publicly released 170-million-parameter small variant of RDT\citep{rdt170mrelease}. \mbox{RDT-1B} names the larger, billion-parameter model\citep{liu2024rdt}. Each cohort contains \RescoreEpisodes{} episodes with \CallsPerEpisode{} calls, using environment-seed blocks 42 and 142 with 15 episodes each. These are environment seeds, not training runs. Every episode reaches the 400-step, 25-call cap without success.

Shared environment seeds and episode indices give the same seeded initial-state setup. Yet vision $B$ differs in all \CohortUnequalBaselines{} aligned calls. Its cross-cohort log correlation is \CohortInitialCorrelation{} at call 0, drops to 0.289 by call 2, then varies between about 0.4 and 0.8, ending at \CohortFinalCorrelation{} at call 24. Similar vision summaries thus come from related trajectories. Missing original contexts prevent assigning differences to particular inputs or noise. The cohorts support separate response comparisons under different ranking targets, not a comparison of ranking quality.

Both full-episode PickCube cohorts passed the rescoring procedure described below. A separate short verification set, distinct from the numerical collection, retains only calls 0--3 across four tasks, or 0--6 for PegInsertionSide. Full-episode $m=128$ PegInsertionSide/\mbox{PickSingleYCB} and fine-tuned \mbox{RDT-1B} runs each use one ranking target and remain unprocessed by this procedure. A single target still permits rescoring, a direct next test. \mbox{RDT-1B} language baselines differ across seeds, so only vision curves are comparable across seeds. Records from a third ranking target, maximum coordinate deviation, are excluded because their stored scores cannot recover squared residuals. Table~\ref{tab:sources} links sources to claims.

Each cohort stored curves scored with its own ranking function. We recover $r=-2d_aQ$ or $r=N^2-\epsilon$ from those values, then recompute both responses, endpoints and AUCs. N-ranked actual-input endpoints store approximately $-10^{-6}$, whose inversion gives a tiny negative residual from roundoff. We remove 24 duplicate records that differ only in timing, leaving every distinct saved call.

\subsection{Descriptive statistical analysis}

The response identity predicts a nonnegative per-call difference for fully in-range curves. Medians and positive-call shares describe the typical effect, while means retain its tail magnitude. We chose this emphasis after examining the data. We report all eight combinations of cohort, modality and direction, which reuse observations and are dependent.

Descriptive 95\% percentile intervals use \BootstrapDraws{} whole-episode bootstrap samples with seed 0. Equal-episode and equal-call means coincide because every episode has \CallsPerEpisode{} calls. Intervals describe logged episodes, not independently trained models. We split means into AUC-weighted in-range and overshoot contributions and count single-episode omissions preserving their sign. No finite AUC or paired-difference values are clipped or trimmed.

\subsection{Numerical diagnostic procedure}

The numerical sample tests a separate offline RDT function recomputed in 32-bit floating point (fp32) from cached encoder outputs. Weights and adapted image/language values retain bf16-rounded values lifted to fp32; state is readapted in fp32. Its source is a separate collection capped at 13 policy calls per episode, which preserved noise. A new reference action is computed with that noise held fixed. Calls 0 and 12 were selected prospectively to cover the initial and later portions of this short collection. Of \NumericalPlanned{} planned contexts, including \NumericalPlannedOneB{} for \mbox{RDT-1B}, \NumericalComplete{} are complete, 9 unfinished and 3 have selected calls that were never reached.

IG budgets are 128, 256 and 512 intervals for vision and language, and 256, 512 and 1,024 for state. Finest maps were computed twice; gradients were evaluated three times at seven fixed path positions. Equality checks compare repeated gradients, maps and responses; coverage checks require the planned positions, budgets, repeats and response comparisons to be present. These are dependent checks on the same contexts. Using the trapezoidal estimator $A_{i,m}=A_{i,m}^{\mathrm{trap}}$, the relative completeness residual $E_m$ and relative coordinate change $D_m$ are
\begin{equation}
E_m=\frac{|\sum_i A_{i,m}-(F(x)-F(x'))|}{|F(x)-F(x')|},\qquad
D_m=\frac{\|A_m-A_{2m}\|_1}{\|A_{2m}\|_1}.
\label{eq:diagnostics}
\end{equation}
Zero denominators are undefined. Absolute endpoint changes at or below $10^{-12}$ for Q or $10^{-8}$ for N and root-mean-square action discrepancy (RMS) are flagged near-zero. Completeness at most 1\% is required if a budget is selected and its endpoint change exceeds the cutoff; no budget is selected here. Mean path-gradient maps omit the IG factor $x-x'$. Both map comparisons require relative coordinate $L_1$ change at most 1\%, Spearman at least 0.99 for nonconstant group scores, and top-five-percent overlap at least 0.95, replaced by matching top-one for an empty subset. Maximum RMS-curve and absolute raw RMS-area changes must be at most 1\% and 0.5\% of all-baseline RMS, respectively. Absolute normalized-AUC changes must be at most 0.01 under each common Q, N or RMS response when its endpoint gap exceeds the cutoff; Section~\ref{sec:numerical-results}'s examples use Q. These are sensitivity criteria, not exact-IG error bounds. Budget-check violations count all failed comparisons and conditional completeness checks, including coarse budgets. Finest-pair counts include a context when any IG or mean path-gradient comparison between its two largest budgets fails.

\subsection{Ethical considerations}

This study used simulated robot policies and saved numerical records. There were no human or animal subjects.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Scoring with N instead of Q cannot increase in-range AUC}

For squared residual $r$, Equation~\ref{eq:normalization} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_Q(r)&=1-r/B,\\
C_N(r)&=\frac{\sqrt{B+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{B+\epsilon}-\sqrt{\epsilon}},\\
C_Q(r)-C_N(r)&=\frac{(\sqrt{r+\epsilon}-\sqrt{\epsilon})(\sqrt{B+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon})}{B}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:response-identity}
\end{equation}
The difference is positive for $0<r<B$, zero at endpoints and negative at overshoots (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). Thus N cannot increase either fully in-range AUC, and the decrease is strict if some point with positive AUC weight has $0<r<B$. Curve monotonicity is unnecessary. As $\epsilon\to0$, setting $t=r/B$ gives $C_Q=1-t$ and $C_N=1-\sqrt t$. More generally, an increasing convex rescaling lies below the normalized endpoint chord in range; N is such a rescaling of Q. A strict decrease makes the fixed ranking look worse by insertion and better by deletion.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{figures_revision/response_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The response difference $C_Q-C_N$ changes sign at overshoot.} \textbf{A)} Normalized responses versus $r/B$, with $B=1$, $\epsilon=10^{-12}$. Rug: saved nodes from the Q-ranked vision-insertion call nearest the median difference. \textbf{B)} Extreme call from Section~\ref{sec:overshoots}; negative values mark overshoot. Blue: Q; orange: N. The symmetric logarithmic scale is linear for $|C_S|\le1$.}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

The pointwise difference is at most $1/4$, including at overshoots, which only lower it. Consequently every defined saved curve satisfies the pointwise-derived bound $\Delta\le1/4$. On the nine-point schedule, zero endpoint differences carry 13\% of the AUC weight, giving the tighter schedule bound \ScheduleDifferenceBound{} (Appendix~\ref{sec:response-proof}). Fully in-range curves additionally have $\Delta\ge0$. These results hold for any fixed ranking, independently of how accurately IG was computed.

A random-order reference is the expected AUC of a random feature ordering, often assumed to be 0.5. Consider an additive scalar policy summing $n$ equal input features, with all-one input and zero baseline. For $n=100$, every ordering gives insertion and deletion AUC about 0.660 under Q on the nine-point schedule, versus 0.5 under N with $\epsilon=0$. On all $n+1$ whole-feature fractions, both quadratic AUCs are $2/3-1/(6n^2)$. Squaring the discrepancy creates interactions even for an additive policy\citep{hama2023deletion}. These are analytic calibration values, not measured random rankings on RDT (Appendix~\ref{sec:random-calibration}). Subtracting them from the opening deletion medians would put both about 0.21 below their respective references. That arithmetic illustrates why an absolute-bar flip need not survive response-matched calibration, and why the actual reference must be measured for each response.

\subsection{Saved curves show a substantial response-only shift}

Within the Q-ranked cohort, changing only the response from Q to N lowers median vision-deletion AUC from \RescoreQDeletionQ{} to \RescoreQDeletionL{}. Within the N-ranked cohort, changing from N to Q raises it from \RescoreLDeletionL{} to \RescoreLDeletionQ{}. Both show a substantial change while the ranked interventions remain fixed.

All eight paired medians are positive, from \PairedMedianMin{} to \PairedMedianMax{}, and \PositiveShareMin{}\% to \PositiveShareMax{}\% of calls have positive differences (Figure~\ref{fig:paired}). In each cohort, these shifts are comparable to the roughly 0.16 shift that a response change gives every ordering of the additive calibration. Fully in-range calls account for \FullyInRangeShareMin{}\% to \FullyInRangeShareMax{}\% across cases; all are positive in these data. Among calls with an overshoot, \OvershootPositiveShareMin{}\% to \OvershootPositiveShareMax{}\% still have positive differences. These measured proportions, together with the identity, explain the positive medians.

First-call vision-insertion medians are $\QVisionInsertionFirstMedian{}$ for Q-ranked and $\NormVisionInsertionFirstMedian{}$ for N-ranked calls, whereas both later-call medians are positive. The largest positive difference, about \MaxPositiveChange{}, reaches \MaxPositiveBoundPercent{}\% of the schedule bound. Among fully in-range Q-ranked vision-insertion calls, the middle half of paired differences spans \QVisionInsertionInRangeQuartileLow{} to \QVisionInsertionInRangeQuartileHigh{}: the response shift is not a constant offset.

\subsection{Overshoots can dominate means through extreme calls}
\label{sec:overshoots}

Overshoot points occur in \OvershootShareMin{}\% to \OvershootShareMax{}\% of calls. The in-range contribution is nonnegative by construction, but negative overshoot contributions reverse six of eight means despite positive medians (Table~\ref{tab:paired}). \subsection{Changing the target can reverse exact IG rankings}

Changing the target poses a different problem even for exact integration. Where gradients exist,
\begin{equation}
\nabla Q(u)=\frac{\sqrt{r(u)+\epsilon}}{d_a}\nabla N(u).
\label{eq:path-weight}
\end{equation}
The factor varies along the path and reweights coordinate contributions. For $g(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+\tfrac45x_2$, baseline $(0,0)$, input $(1,1)$, reference $9/5$ and $d_a=1$, exact Q attributions are $(23/30,64/75)$, ranking feature 2 first. N ranks feature 1 first by about 0.2 (Figure~\ref{fig:toys}A; Appendix~\ref{sec:reversal-proof}). In this example, Q's weight on $\nabla g$ decreases to zero along the path, suppressing feature 1's growing gradient. The factor in Equation~\ref{eq:path-weight} itself remains positive at the input for positive $\epsilon$. This is an existence example; its path-weighting mechanism is specific to IG, whereas squared-discrepancy masking scores also occur in policy analysis\citep{zhang2026embodied}.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Discussion}

For question one, response choice substantially changes fixed-ranking evaluations; overshoots can dominate means. For question two, finite numerical agreement does not certify integration. Question three, ranking utility, remains unresolved. Separately, attribution target changes can reverse exact feature order.

Normalization puts calls on a common scale but amplifies small all-baseline squared residuals. Hama and colleagues instead use unnormalized areas from the straight endpoint reference, avoiding this division while retaining response units\citep{hama2023deletion}. On their full prefix grid, combining insertion with deletion oriented so larger means better gives zero random-order expectation even with interactions; either area alone need not. Their IG argument gives an optimal feature order for an increasing transform of a linear inner score, whose gradient direction is constant. Appendix~\ref{sec:reversal-proof}'s nonlinear inner score falls outside that condition: feature 1 first is better under both responses, yet exact Q-target IG ranks feature 2 first.

\subsection{A practical reporting procedure}
\label{sec:procedure}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Specify the attribution target separately from the scoring response, with the reference action, noise, feature groups, baseline and intervention schedule. The target-reversal example and response identity show why both choices matter.
\item Compare rankings in matched contexts under the same response, including response-matched random rankings or a simple fixed feature order. A common response change can reverse two rankings (Appendix~\ref{sec:ranking-comparison}); justify the response or check both.
\item Keep interventions fixed when comparing responses. Report undefined normalization when $B=0$, overshoots, distributions of $B$ or unnormalized areas, medians, positive shares and full tails alongside means. For repeated calls from episodes, resample whole episodes.
\item Check repeatability, coordinate changes and completeness across budgets; separately report ranking and curve changes. Keep IG and mean path-gradient results distinct because multiplication by $x-x'$ can change rankings. Report the completed and planned context counts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and next steps}

The headline evidence is one simulated task, one model recorded as pretrained \mbox{RDT-170M}, two environment-seed blocks and 30 episodes per cohort, with no successes. A failing policy still tests a scoring-only identity, but its effect sizes and overshoot rates may not generalize. Existing fine-tuned \mbox{RDT-1B} PickCube runs succeed in \OneBSuccesses{} of \OneBEpisodes{} episodes. Rescoring their vision curves through the same checked procedure is the most direct available test with a more competent policy; additional tasks and physical robots would further test generality.

Normalized AUC is unbounded below at overshoots. No matched random or fixed-order control establishes ranking superiority, and no model-weight randomization test is performed\citep{adebayo2018sanity}. The incomplete numerical sample has no completed \mbox{RDT-1B} context and cannot estimate how often numerical checks fail across policy contexts. It does not validate the original bf16 rankings: the saved PickCube runs lack original observations, noise arrays and exact checkpoint files. Future studies should preserve complete contexts and compare finer or adaptive integration against explicitly justified error controls.

\subsection{Conclusion}

N cannot increase either fully in-range AUC, and the saved PickCube evaluations show substantial response-only changes. Strict decreases favor deletion and disfavor insertion; absolute thresholds can give opposite verdicts. Overshoots can dominate means, especially through extreme first calls in vision insertion. Target choice can reverse exact IG rankings, and numerical checks reveal problems without certifying accuracy. Report targets and responses separately, compare rankings with matched controls, retain full distributions, and check numerical sensitivity.

\section{Response identities and target-induced reversal}
\label{sec:proofs}

\subsection{Normalized response difference}
\label{sec:response-proof}

Assume exact self-reference and $B>0$. Set $\eta=\sqrt{\epsilon}$, $b=\sqrt{B+\epsilon}$ and $z=\sqrt{r+\epsilon}$, so $B=(b-\eta)(b+\eta)$. Then
\begin{equation}
C_Q-C_N=\frac{b^2-z^2}{B}-\frac{b-z}{b-\eta}
=\frac{(z-\eta)(b-z)}{B}.
\end{equation}
For $\eta\le z\le b$, the product is nonnegative and maximized at $z=(\eta+b)/2$, giving
\begin{equation}
0\le C_Q-C_N\le\frac{(b-\eta)^2}{4B}
=\frac{b-\eta}{4(b+\eta)}\le\frac14.
\end{equation}
For $z>b$, the difference is negative, so the $1/4$ upper bound holds for all $r\ge0$. Nonnegative AUC weights sum to one, yielding $\Delta\le1/4$ for every defined curve. On the nine-point schedule they are $(0.005,0.025,0.045,0.075,0.10,0.15,0.225,0.25,0.125)$. The endpoints contribute zero difference and have total weight 0.13, giving $\Delta\le0.87/4=0.2175$. For positive $\epsilon$ and finite $B$, the fixed-$B$ schedule bound $0.87(b-\eta)/[4(b+\eta)]$ is strictly smaller. Fully in-range curves additionally satisfy $\Delta\ge0$, strictly if a point with positive AUC weight has $0<r<B$. If $B=0$, normalization is undefined. When the 1\% and 5\% language values coincide, dropping the 1\% node changes their combined AUC weight by 0.02; the $1/4$ pointwise bound therefore bounds the paired change by $0.02/4=0.005$ for fully in-range calls.

\subsection{Finite-stabilizer rank reversal}
\label{sec:reversal-proof}

Let $g(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+\beta x_2$, with $\beta=4/5$, $x'=(0,0)$ and $x=(1,1)$. Along the diagonal, the scalar action residual and its square are
\[
\rho(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha+\beta),\qquad r(\alpha,\alpha)=\rho(\alpha)^2.
\]
Since $\nabla g=(2\alpha,\beta)$ there, differentiating the targets for $d_a=1$ gives
\begin{equation}
\nabla Q=\rho(\alpha)(2\alpha,\beta),\qquad
\nabla N=\frac{\rho(\alpha)}{\sqrt{\rho(\alpha)^2+\epsilon}}(2\alpha,\beta).
\end{equation}
Polynomial integration yields
\begin{equation}
A^Q=\left(\frac12+\frac\beta3,\frac{2\beta}3+\frac{\beta^2}2\right)
=\left(\frac{23}{30},\frac{64}{75}\right),
\end{equation}
so feature 2 leads by $13/150$. Norm attributions tend to $(1,\beta)$ as $\epsilon$ decreases to zero.

For the positive stabilizer, define $\psi(\rho)=1-\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2+\epsilon}\ge0$. Its derivative is $-\epsilon/(\rho^2+\epsilon)^{3/2}<0$, and $\rho(\alpha)\ge\beta(1-\alpha)$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1\psi(\rho(\alpha))\,d\alpha
&\le\int_0^1\left[1-\frac{\beta(1-\alpha)}{\sqrt{\beta^2(1-\alpha)^2+\epsilon}}\right]d\alpha\\
&=1-\frac{\sqrt{\beta^2+\epsilon}-\sqrt{\epsilon}}{\beta}
\le\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{\beta}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The identities $1-A_1^N=\int_0^1 2\alpha\psi(\rho(\alpha))\,d\alpha$ and $\beta-A_2^N=\beta\int_0^1\psi(\rho(\alpha))\,d\alpha$, together with $0\le2\alpha\le2$ and $\epsilon=10^{-12}$, give
\begin{equation}
0\le1-A_1^N\le2.5\times10^{-6},\qquad
0\le\beta-A_2^N\le10^{-6}.
\end{equation}
Thus $A_1^N-A_2^N\ge0.1999975$. Every coordinate is positive, so ordinary and absolute-coordinate rankings agree. Only the target changes. At the half-feature insertion point, feature 1 first gives normalized Q response about 0.80 versus 0.69 for feature 2 first, and N response about 0.56 versus 0.44. Deletion swaps these intermediate values. Hence feature 1 first scores better under both responses, despite exact Q-target IG ranking feature 2 first.

\subsection{Finite-grid random-order calibration}
\label{sec:random-calibration}

For $g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$, an all-one input and zero baseline, every feature order gives residual distances $k$ after $k$ deletions and $n-k$ after $k$ insertions. At $q=k/n$, normalized Q curves are $1-q^2$ and $1-(1-q)^2$. Both have second derivative $-2$, so their trapezoidal areas underestimate their common integral $2/3$ by $1/(6n^2)$ on the full-prefix grid. Their identical curvature also gives identical quadrature errors on any common grid, including an asymmetric one. The nine-point schedule with $n=100$ gives area 0.65976. Unstabilized N gives the lines $1-q$ and $q$, both with trapezoidal area 0.5 on either schedule.

\subsection{A common response change can reorder rankings}
\label{sec:ranking-comparison}

Take three features and insertion fractions $\{0,1/3,2/3,1\}$, with $B=1$. Let orders $(1,2,3)$ and $(3,2,1)$ give residual sequences $(1,1/4,1/4,0)$ and $(1,16/25,0,0)$, respectively. Their intermediate subsets are distinct, so these values can belong to one scalar policy with the same endpoints. Q insertion AUCs are $2/3$ and $31/50=0.620$; with $\epsilon=0$, N AUCs are $1/2$ and $17/30\approx0.567$. Thus the better order reverses. The inequalities persist at $\epsilon=10^{-12}$. This establishes possibility, not a measured comparison of the saved cohorts.
