\begin{abstract}
Attribution rankings select inputs for interventions, and their evaluations also need responses that score those interventions.
We study this distinction for Integrated Gradients (IG) attributions of an action chunk predicted by a diffusion policy with fixed noise.
For quadratic and stabilized negative-$\ell_2$ action-discrepancy targets, we give an exact relation between their normalized responses.
We also derive a positive, path-dependent factor between their gradients.
Using a smooth construction, we show that IG rankings can reverse while the nonzero gradients remain pointwise collinear.
A finite-grid counterexample shows that 0.5 cannot serve as a universal random-ranking AUC reference.
Reanalyzing \ArchiveFiles{} preserved Robotics Diffusion Transformer files, we find that changing only the evaluation response changes median vision deletion AUC from \RescoreQDeletionQ{} to \RescoreQDeletionL{} although the recorded quadratic-target rankings stay the same.
In all eight paired rescoring cases, every median response change is positive although six means are negative because of overshooting tails.
Exploratory episode-omission results retain insertion-mean signs but can reverse language-deletion signs.
Another smooth construction gives several integration grids with the same completeness, ranking and perturbation curves but an ordering that reverses the exact IG order.
In this example, the inaccurate ordering scores better on both perturbation metrics.
Diagnostics on the actual model separate repeatability from unresolved integration-budget sensitivity in an incomplete cohort.
Together, the results separate response-geometry effects from attribution-ranking quality and numerical-integration accuracy.
They do not qualify a production numerical setting, demonstrate better attribution rankings, or explain task success or dependence on learned weights.
The artifact retains raw-record lineage, analytic checks, adverse results and provenance limits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) models like RT-2~\citep{zitkovich2023rt2}, Octo~\citep{team2024octo}, and the Robotics Diffusion Transformer (RDT)~\citep{liu2024rdt} use robot observations along with language instructions to predict robot actions.\footnote{AI tools were used to develop and check mathematical constructions, design and implement saved-data analyses, review code and literature, and draft and revise this manuscript. The author is responsible for the content.}
Diffusion policies~\citep{chi2023diffusion} iteratively remove noise~\citep{ho2020ddpm} to produce action chunks.
Applying a scalar attribution method to this vector output requires deciding which aspect of the action prediction to explain.
This decision also affects how the explanation is evaluated.

Integrated Gradients (IG) integrates the gradient of a scalar output along a path between a baseline and an input~\citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}.
For a fixed-noise action sample, the action chunk's discrepancy from a detached reference can define this scalar function.
A quadratic discrepancy and a negative norm both measure change, but they are different scalar functions.
Evaluating the attribution also requires a response that scores interventions.
The ranking selects which features to remove or insert, while the response measures the effect of those changes.
If both the scalar target and evaluation response change, native-response AUC alone cannot determine whether the ranking has improved.

We analyze these issues through exact derivations, constructive examples and a retrospective RDT case study.
For fixed selected features and perturbed outputs, changing the response from a quadratic discrepancy to a stabilized negative $\ell_2$ discrepancy changes normalized AUC.
At the same time, the positive scaling factor between the targets' gradients varies along the path, which allows their IG rankings to differ.
Rescoring interventions is therefore a distinct analysis from recomputing attributions.
Neither result shows which ranking is more useful when the response is held fixed.

We also ask whether the computed attribution accurately approximates the specified mathematical quantity.
Small scalar completeness residuals can hide errors in individual input coordinates.
The same inaccurate IG approximation can also appear when a finite-precision calculation is repeated.
The smooth construction in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aliasing} gives nested grids that agree exactly on completeness, ranking and perturbation responses while exact IG has the reversed ranking.
Our diagnostics report the checks already collected together with the full planned roster and its unfinished cells.
Passing checks in part of this roster does not certify the implementation.
The intended attributions measure action discrepancy at a fixed context.
They do not explain eventual task success or ensure safe robot behavior.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Response and path-weight analysis.} We derive an identity for normalized responses with precise qualifications for overshoot and zero-gap cases. We also give a constructive example with a finite stabilizer in which the quadratic and negative-norm targets reverse IG feature order.
\item \textbf{A finite-grid calibration counterexample.} A policy with 100 equally contributing features has quadratic-response insertion and deletion AUC equal to \RandomGridAUC{} for all orderings on the historical evaluation grid. This shows why 0.5 is not a universal chance reference.
\item \textbf{An authenticated retrospective case study.} We rescore saved RDT perturbation curves while holding feature rankings fixed. The analysis records the occurrence rules, episode grouping and links to the underlying source records. Unknown historical context identities prevent a paired comparison of ranking quality.
\item \textbf{Numerical diagnostic limits.} We give a smooth counterexample to certification by finite-grid agreement and report an incomplete campaign of fixed-input repeatability and integration-budget checks. Check failures and incomplete coverage remain visible. We do not certify settings for production use.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these contributions separate the issues and document their consequences in one diffusion-policy family.
A ranking study that holds the response fixed would address the additional question of ranking efficacy.
The absence of that study limits what we can conclude about efficacy, but it does not invalidate the analysis that holds rankings fixed.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Attribution Methods}

\citet{sundararajan2017axiomatic} introduced IG with the sensitivity and implementation-invariance axioms, demonstrating that straight-line path integration satisfies both.
Subsequent work addresses limitations of the original method.
\citet{jha2021smoother} computed attributions over neural stochastic differential equation models for smoother maps.
Their later work improved robustness by shaping the injected noise~\citep{jha2022shaping}.
\citet{walker2024idg} showed that gradients in saturation regions add noise and proposed Integrated Decision Gradients, which puts more of the integration on the part of the path relevant to the model's decision.
For vision transformers, metric-driven attributions~\citep{walker2025metric} and information-flow decompositions~\citep{walker2025infoflow} improve patch-level explanations.
Guided IG~\citep{kapishnikov2021guided} changes the integration path to reduce noise in pixel-level attributions.

Several works analyze sensitivity to the integration budget.
\citet{makino2024integration} examine instance-varying completeness residuals, nonmonotone changes with step count and unresolved gradients along the IG path.
\citet{singhi2024strengthening} analyze step-count sensitivity in IG variants and the Important Direction Gradient Integration (IDGI) framework.
Their reported stability experiment compares JPEG-compressed and uncompressed inputs.
Our diagnostics instead keep the context fixed and analyze repeatability separately from changes in attribution coordinates across integration budgets.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-aliasing} gives a smooth construction that shows a limit of these checks.
Agreement on finitely many integration grids together with exact scalar completeness does not imply accurate coordinate integrals.

\citet{petsiuk2018rise} proposed the insertion and deletion curves that we use as ranking-based faithfulness metrics.
Several works analyze the reliability of perturbation metrics.
\citet{hooker2019roar} evaluate attributions by ablating inputs and retraining the model.
\citet{rong2022road} identify information leakage through the shape of the mask when deletion is performed without retraining.
\citet{tomsett2020sanity} find common saliency metrics to be unreliable.
This literature already establishes that the evaluation depends on the metric.
Our case study examines this dependence for action-discrepancy responses through an algebraic relationship between normalized curves and by rescoring preserved interventions with their feature ordering fixed.
\citet{adebayo2018sanity} introduced model and data randomization tests to check whether saliency maps depend on the model's trained parameters and training data.
These checks show that even visually plausible attributions do not establish that dependence.
Our backbone randomizations are limited because the learned encoders and adaptors are kept in place.
For image diffusion models, cross-attention maps relate generated pixels to specific prompt tokens~\citep{tang2023daam}.
This is different from attributing the scalar discrepancy of a predicted control chunk.

Our implementation uses IG with modality-specific baselines to attribute a fixed-noise action-discrepancy function.
Path weighting is already used in methods such as IDG~\citep{walker2024idg}.
The factor we derive follows specifically from changing the scalar discrepancy target.
We apply these established concerns to quadratic and stabilized negative-norm action-discrepancy functions and examine their separate effects on fixed interventions and integrated rankings.

\subsection{Vision-Language-Action Models}

VLA models combine perception, language understanding and action generation.
RT-2~\citep{zitkovich2023rt2} fine-tunes a vision-language model to predict actions that are discretized and represented as token sequences.
Octo~\citep{team2024octo} is a transformer-based generalist policy trained on a large multi-robot dataset.
Diffusion Policy~\citep{chi2023diffusion} formulates robot action generation as conditional denoising diffusion~\citep{ho2020ddpm}.
Action chunks are generated through iterative refinement instead of single-step regression.
RDT~\citep{liu2024rdt} scales this diffusion-based action prediction using a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone, a SigLIP image encoder~\citep{zhai2023siglip}, and a T5-XXL text encoder~\citep{raffel2020t5}.
Each frame from three cameras is encoded through SigLIP.
A history of two frames gives six camera slots and produces 4{,}374 vision tokens.
These are combined with up to 1{,}024 language tokens and a 128-dimensional unified state vector.
The model then generates chunks of 64 action steps with a five-step denoising schedule using DPM-Solver++~\citep{lu2022dpm}.
The multiple modalities and repeated denoising steps make RDT an interesting but challenging model for attribution.

\subsection{Interpretability in Robotics}

Earlier work on robot-policy explanations focused on reinforcement learning agents in discrete-action settings.
\citet{greydanus2018visualizing} studied perturbation-based saliency in the Atari domain.
Counterfactual methods~\citep{amitai2024explaining} explain policies by comparing what happens under executed actions with what would happen under alternatives.
VisualBackProp~\citep{bojarski2018visualbackprop} visualizes convolutional neural network (CNN) saliency for end-to-end driving policies in continuous control.
These are convolutional driving models rather than multimodal diffusion samplers.
Recent VLA methods examine different quantities to explain.
\citet{zhang2026embodied} estimate visual influence through randomized perturbations and action-discrepancy responses, with explicit assumptions relating mean-squared error to distributional divergence.
VLA-Trace~\citep{shi2026vlatrace} combines representation-level comparisons, attention interventions and behavioral probes.
IMPACT-VLA~\citep{kim2026impact} attributes phase-modality interventions to downstream task success through counterfactual closed-loop execution.
Our estimand is the discrepancy of a predicted action chunk under fixed noise and at a fixed context.
It does not explain eventual task success.
We focus on how response geometry affects the evaluation of IG rankings for this estimand and make no claim to originate VLA interpretability.

\section{Attribution Targets and Evaluation}

\subsection{How Integrated Gradients Is Computed}

For a differentiable function $F \!:\! \mathbb{R}^n \!\to\! \mathbb{R}$, input $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and baseline $x' \in \mathbb{R}^n$, Integrated Gradients follows the straight-line path from the baseline to the input.
The contribution of dimension $i$ is computed by integrating the gradient with respect to that dimension,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}_i(x, x') = (x_i - x'_i) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\bigg|_{x' + \alpha(x - x')} d\alpha .
\label{eq:ig}
\end{equation}
Under conditions where the fundamental theorem of calculus and the chain rule apply along the path, IG obeys \emph{completeness}.
This means that $\sum_i \mathrm{IG}_i(x, x') = F(x) - F(x')$.
We approximate the integral as a uniform Riemann sum with $m$ steps,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}_i \approx \frac{x_i - x'_i}{m+1} \sum_{r=0}^{m} \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\bigg|_{x' + \frac{r}{m}(x - x')} .
\label{eq:riemann}
\end{equation}
In the historical implementation, all terms receive equal weight, including both endpoints of the integration path.
Convergence of this approximation to exact IG depends on how well-behaved the function being integrated is along the path.
Summation with finite numerical precision creates an additional source of error.
Increasing $m$ does not distinguish this error from integration error or necessarily make it smaller.

\subsection{Keeping the Target Fixed Through Denoising}
\label{sec:target}

At policy call $t$, the diffusion sampler output is $g_\theta(o_t,z_t)$, a function of the observation $o_t$ and fixed diffusion noise $z_t$.
We write this predicted action chunk as $\mu_t$ to maintain consistency with historical records.
It is a sample obtained with fixed diffusion noise.
This notation alone does not identify it as a conditional mean.
We compute the reference $a_t=g_\theta(o_t,z_t)$ once without gradients.
Under an intervention, we use the auxiliary attribution target
\begin{equation}
Q_t=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2D}\lVert a_t-\mu_t\rVert^2,
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^2=1$ and $D=512$ active entries cover 64 predicted time steps over eight active dimensions.
The score is the portion of a Gaussian log kernel that depends on the action difference, averaged over entries and excluding the log-normalizing constant.
It has not been derived as the conditional log likelihood of the diffusion policy.
Historical code and files refer to this target as \texttt{logpi}, and its change is written as $\Delta Q$.
While the execution loop acts only on a subsampled prefix of the chunk before stopping or calling the policy again, we compute attributions for the whole predicted chunk.

We keep the noise realization fixed across points along the integration path so that the target remains a deterministic function of the input.
An expectation over noise would be a different quantity to attribute.
Explaining it would require justification for switching the order of the gradient and expectation, along with a Monte Carlo treatment.
Using a new independent noise sample at every path point does not compute the fixed-noise quantity studied here.
In the historical implementation, nonfinite gradient components were replaced by zeros.
This could make individual IG coordinates wrong even when their errors cancel in the sum.
That cancellation can leave the scalar completeness residual small or zero.
Passing a completeness check therefore does not mean that each coordinate is correct.

\subsection{Other Scalar Targets}
\label{sec:targets}

We compare the quadratic target with two other targets for the action difference, given by
\begin{equation}
y_t^{\ell_2}=-\sqrt{\lVert a_t-\mu_t\rVert_2^2+\epsilon},
\qquad y_t^{\mathrm{max}}=-\max_j|a_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j}|,
\end{equation}
For our analyses, the stabilizer used in the implementation is $\epsilon=10^{-12}$.
Both targets use the same $D$ active entries.
The stabilized $\ell_2$ target is a smooth function of the residual and has zero gradient where the predicted and reference actions are identical.
Away from the stabilizer scale, its gradient with respect to the actions approaches unit norm.
The maximum-deviation target, which we call maxdev, measures the action difference using a different norm.
It is not generally a monotone transformation of $\ell_2$.
For example, the residual vectors $(1,1)$ and $(1.1,0)$ reverse order under these norms.
For a fixed perturbation, the quadratic and stabilized $\ell_2$ responses have an algebraic relationship, but their gradients can still give different attribution rankings.
Differences in scale or units do not imply that a target with a larger magnitude contains more information or is more useful.
Section~\ref{sec:targets-results} focuses on response changes between the quadratic and stabilized $\ell_2$ targets.
Records for maxdev remain available in the released analysis as an additional diagnostic.

\subsection{How the Response Changes the Weight Along the Path}
\label{sec:path-weights}

We fix the reference action, active coordinates, model, noise and other modalities.
We then define $r(x)=\lVert a-\mu(x)\rVert_2^2$ and $L(x)=-\sqrt{r(x)+\epsilon}$.
By the chain rule, wherever the gradients exist,
\begin{equation}
\nabla Q(x)=\frac{\sqrt{r(x)+\epsilon}}{D\sigma^2}\nabla L(x).
\label{eq:gradient-weight}
\end{equation}
The nonzero gradients of the quadratic and stabilized targets therefore have the same direction at each point.
Both targets' gradients are zero when the prediction exactly matches its own reference.
These facts alone do not determine what happens when the gradients are integrated.
Along a straight-line path $x(\alpha)=x'+\alpha(x-x')$, the integrated gradient is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}^{Q}_i=(x_i-x_i')\int_0^1
\frac{\sqrt{r(x(\alpha))+\epsilon}}{D\sigma^2}
\partial_i L(x(\alpha))\,d\alpha.
\label{eq:weighted-ig}
\end{equation}
The weights are positive but generally vary with $\alpha$.
Different coordinates can contribute more in different regions of the path.
The change in weighting can therefore change the ranking after integration.
This does not conflict with IG implementation invariance, which compares different implementations of the same scalar function~\citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}.

For an example, consider the policy with scalar output $\mu(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+\tfrac45x_2$, baseline $(0,0)$, actual input $(1,1)$, detached reference $a=\tfrac95$, and $D=\sigma^2=1$.
Performing the integration exactly along the same straight-line path gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}^{Q}=\left(\frac{23}{30},\frac{64}{75}\right),
\qquad
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}\mathrm{IG}^{L}=\left(1,\frac45\right).
\label{eq:rank-reversal}
\end{equation}
The quadratic target ranks feature 2 first.
The stabilized $L$ target with $\epsilon=10^{-12}$ ranks feature 1 first, by a margin of at least $0.1999975$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:geometry-proofs} proves this bound using the finite stabilizer.
The reversal therefore holds for the implemented response and does not rely on replacing it with an idealized limiting value.
This shows that rankings can reverse in a smooth nonlinear policy.
It does not show that this happens for all such policies or that it occurs empirically in RDT.

\subsection{Attribution at Each Policy Call}

RDT predicts a chunk of 64 action steps at a time, subsampled $4\times$ to 16 steps in the environment.
IG is performed once per \emph{policy call}, since all actions in the chunk result from a single decision.
We attribute one policy query rather than each environment step.
The wall-clock runtime of IG depends on implementation details, hardware, numerical precision and the integration budget.
We do not use the earlier timing summaries as a performance result.

At each policy call, we perform attribution separately on each set of modality inputs, using a corresponding forward function that fixes the remaining inputs.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vision.} Adapted vision tokens are interpolated from baseline to real representations while language and state inputs are fixed.
\item \textbf{Language.} Adapted language tokens are interpolated from a minimal two-token baseline encoded through T5-XXL to the real instruction embedding, while vision and state inputs are fixed.
\item \textbf{State.} The 128-dimensional unified state vector is interpolated from zeros to its real value while vision and language inputs are fixed. For the Franka arm, only eight dimensions are active, covering the seven joints and the gripper. We include the state-adaptor multilayer perceptron (MLP) inside the forward function to preserve the joint structure.
\end{itemize}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:perstep} summarizes how these attributions are computed.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{IG at each policy call with fixed noise. In Section~\ref{sec:setup}, we describe which historical artifacts are available and their limits.}
\label{alg:perstep}
\textbf{Input.} Policy $\pi_\theta$, environment $\mathcal{E}$, integration steps $m$, target $y \in \{Q, \ell_2, \mathrm{maxdev}\}$\\
\textbf{Output.} Attributions $\{A_t^V, A_t^L, A_t^S\}$ at every policy call.
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $o_0 \leftarrow \mathcal{E}.\text{reset}()$
\FOR{policy call $t = 0, 1, \ldots$}
    \STATE Encode $o_t$ into vision $v_t$, language $l_t$, state $s_t$
    \STATE Draw and store $z_t$. Compute $a_t \leftarrow g_\theta(o_t,z_t)$ without gradients
    \STATE Define $F$ from $a_t$, the fixed-noise predicted chunk $\mu_t$, and the chosen target
    \FOR{modality $M \in \{V, L, S\}$}
        \STATE Set baseline $x'_M$, input $x_M$
        \STATE Freeze the other two modalities
        \STATE $A_t^M \leftarrow \text{IG}(F_M, x_M, x'_M, m)$ \hfill Eq.~\ref{eq:riemann}
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Record $A_t^V, A_t^L, A_t^S$ and completeness errors
    \STATE Apply action chunk and advance $\mathcal{E}$ by up to 16 steps, stopping on termination
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Choosing the baseline.}
The vision baseline is a gray image in the external-camera slot, with a uniform value.
The remaining five camera slots contain constant background images and stay fixed at their real values.
IG therefore attributes only to the slot that contains information about the scene.
The language baseline is a minimal two-token representation using the tokenizer's padding and end-of-sequence tokens.
There is no beginning-of-sequence token in T5.
Rather than taking a zero vector as the baseline, we encode these tokens through T5-XXL.
The encoder output is then padded with zeros to the stored embedding length.
The language forward passes attend to the real token span for the task.
This gives an attended baseline consisting of the encoded token pair, with zeros in the remaining positions being attended to.
The GELU-based language adaptor allows a zero vector as an input, but that would be a different representation baseline.
The preliminary effectiveness comparisons cannot be independently reconstructed.
For state, the baseline is a zero vector in normalized joint space.
This choice does not establish that it represents a physically supported state or is useful as an explanation reference.
Appendix~\ref{sec:anchoring} reports the available checks of sensitivity to the vision baseline.

\subsection{How We Evaluate the Attributions}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Completeness.}
For each modality, we hold the other inputs fixed and compute the absolute residual
$|\sum_i\mathrm{IG}_i-(F(x)-F(x'))|$.
We also compute its relative version by dividing by the magnitude of the scalar endpoint gap,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Err}=\frac{|\sum_i\mathrm{IG}_i-(F(x)-F(x'))|}{|F(x)-F(x')|}.
\end{equation}
This relative value is undefined when the signed gap is exactly zero.
If the gap is small but nonzero, division can amplify numerical error.
The historical action-norm filter $\|a_t\|\geq15$ does not bound this denominator.
We retain the filter only to show how changing the included population affects the results.
A small residual indicates agreement in the scalar accounting identity.
It does not rule out errors in individual coordinates that cancel in the sum but still change the attribution map or feature ranking.

\paragraph{Perturbation curves.}
We sum the absolute coordinate attributions within each eligible feature group and rank the groups by these sums.
For vision, the groups are patch tokens from the external camera.
For language, they are the attended instruction tokens.
Deletion sets the features of the first groups in the ranking to their baseline values.
Insertion starts from the baseline input and inserts the original features for the first groups in that ranking.
For a scalar response $R$, the normalized value is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde R(x_k)=\frac{R(x_k)-R(x')}{R(x)-R(x')}.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
We integrate on the fraction grid stored in each record.
The normalized curves and their AUCs are not necessarily within $[0,1]$.
The historical grid requests $\{0,1,5,10,20,30,50,75,100\}\%$ of the eligible feature groups.
For language, rounding the token counts changes the fractions actually used.
The saved curves should therefore not be interpreted as continuous functions sampled at evenly spaced points.
Top-$k$ score changes are $R(x_k)-R(x)$, so they have the units of the scalar response $R$.
We distinguish the attribution target used for ranking from the response used to score the interventions.
The revised tables do not use the earlier study-specific thresholds as tests of effectiveness.

\paragraph{Randomization and baseline checks.}
For C1, we reinitialize model parameters, compute attributions again and correlate them with the originals.
The standard version changes the final action-output projection and recomputes the reference action.
The frozen-reference and backbone-cascade versions instead retain the original reference.
The historical cascade randomly reinitializes all backbone parameter matrices of rank at least two simultaneously.
It retains one-dimensional parameters, encoders and adaptors.
It does not randomize the layers progressively in a cascade.
For C2, we shuffle external-camera pixels as well as language.
If a cache of shuffled pre-T5 language is available, the historical implementation uses it.
Otherwise, it permutes the post-T5 embeddings.
The saved records do not establish which mode was used for a given attribution call.
These tests measure different forms of dependence.
C2 is not the label-randomization test proposed by \citet{adebayo2018sanity}.
The historical implementation computes perturbed maps at $m=16$, while the original maps nominally use $m=64$.
C1 therefore affects the numerical approximation as well as the model weights.
To check baseline sensitivity, we compare black, gray and blurred observation features.

\section{Retrospective Data and Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Scope and traceability.}
The revised analysis checks \ArchiveFiles{} preserved files containing \ArchiveValidRecords{} valid records and \ArchiveQuarantined{} specifically identified corrupt line.
The released inventory identifies each valid physical record by source path, original line number and byte hash.
Some recorded keys occur more than once. There are \ArchiveDuplicateGroups{} such groups, and \ArchiveConflictingGroups{} differ in scientific fields as well as, or instead of, timing metadata.
The last occurrence is used as a declared rule for this retrospective analysis. When records conflict, this rule does not establish that the selected attempt is correct.
A separate sensitivity analysis excludes every key with a conflicting payload to show how those conflicts affect the results.
The released lineage registry maps records and occurrence decisions to population membership, result identifiers and generated manuscript cells.
These links support checks of the preserved bytes. They cannot recover missing checkpoints, observations, action tensors or noise.

\paragraph{Campaigns and models.}
The main reconstructed RDT-170M tables come from the saved Month 7 verification campaign at $m=64$, covering PickCube, StackCube, PegInsertionSide and PickSingleYCB in ManiSkill3~\citep{tao2024maniskill3}.
These experiments are separate from the original main pass, whose inputs are unavailable.
The verification campaign used pretrained RDT-170M, while the saved RDT-1B PickCube campaign used a task-finetuned checkpoint.
The comparison therefore mixes changes in training, trajectories, historical preprocessing and parameter count. It cannot isolate the effect of model scale or competence.
Appendix~\ref{sec:secondary-historical} consequently reports the 1B cohort separately.
The primary tables exclude missing original completeness and standard-sanity results, along with missing early PickCube/StackCube integration-budget results.

\paragraph{Known protocol limits.}
Historical attribution covers the full predicted action chunk, although the simulator executes only a subsampled chunk.
The four-task selection also reflects historical implementation decisions. An attempted TurnFaucet run used the wrong controller and has no documented corrected rerun. Its unsuccessful outcomes therefore do not establish that the model lacked the capability for that task.
Early follow-up jobs replaced encoded padding/end tokens with a zero language baseline, and the PickSingleYCB follow-up used a proxy PickCube instruction embedding.
The pooled 1B language cohort consequently mixes the early baseline with later encoded baselines.
Earlier sidecar filenames omitted seed and integration-budget fields, allowing files to be overwritten and possibly reused across nominal runs.
The seed-tagged Month 7 records reduce this filename problem but cannot recover the old contexts.
Matching scalar fingerprints alone therefore cannot establish exact context equality.

\paragraph{Estimands and uncertainty.}
The primary population includes all valid saved calls after the declared occurrence rule is applied.
Continuous call-level quantities are summarized by medians over calls. Residual pass rates are proportions over the stated call population. Solver contrasts give relative changes between endpoint medians, while the separately named equal-episode analysis uses episode medians.
Uncertainty summaries resample whole recorded episode groups, formed from task, model, recorded seed and episode, with $B=10{,}000$ draws and seed zero.
Each point estimate and its interval use exactly the same source-record population.
The percentile intervals describe the released empirical mixture of episodes. Longer episodes contribute more calls to that mixture. These intervals do not measure variability across independently trained checkpoints.
The separately named 1B sensitivity instead gives each episode equal weight through the median of episode medians.
For the old randomization variants, we also join nominal task/episode keys across recorded seeds when reuse is possible. This checks conditional sensitivity to dependence between those records.
Neither grouping recovers unobserved context identity.
Very small cluster counts are stated explicitly. Resampling two baseline groups, in particular, is not evidence of calibrated interval coverage.
All unfiltered results and the historical norm-selected sensitivity remain available. Tails are not removed to obtain a favorable threshold verdict.

The paired rescoring analysis keeps each call's ranking and interventions fixed. It defines $\Delta_{ej}=\operatorname{AUC}_{Q,ej}-\operatorname{AUC}_{L,ej}$ for call $j$ in recorded episode $e$.
The primary estimand is $E^{-1}\sum_{e=1}^E n_e^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_e}\Delta_{ej}$, giving every episode equal weight. A prespecified descriptive sensitivity uses the median of paired call differences.
Both summarize within-call changes. Neither is the difference between the marginal medians reported above.
The protocol specified all eight ranking/modality/direction combinations and the same $10{,}000$-draw episode-bootstrap convention before these summaries were computed.
Its intervals are conditional descriptions without a multiplicity adjustment. They do not establish calibrated joint coverage or global significance.
If either response has a zero endpoint gap, the paired difference remains undefined and stays in the accounting. All 750 calls in each ranking cohort have defined paired differences, and each of the 30 recorded episodes contributes 25 calls. The equal-episode and equal-call means therefore coincide here.

\section{Response Geometry and Retrospective Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Separating Attribution Target from Evaluation Response}
\label{sec:targets-results}

A recorded intervention can be scored under another response without recomputing IG or changing any selected feature.
Write the recorded active-action discrepancy as $d$, with $Q=-d^2/1024$, and $L=-\sqrt{d^2+\epsilon}$ with $\epsilon=10^{-12}$.
The two responses are related by
\begin{equation}
L=-\sqrt{-1024Q+\epsilon},\qquad Q=-\frac{L^2-\epsilon}{1024}.
\label{eq:rescore}
\end{equation}
Under exact self-reference at the actual endpoint, if the all-baseline squared residual is $R>0$, the normalized values satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
N_Q(r)&=1-\frac rR,\qquad
N_L(r)=\frac{\sqrt{R+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{R+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon},\\
N_Q(r)-N_L(r)&=\frac{(\sqrt{r+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon)(\sqrt{R+\epsilon}-\sqrt{r+\epsilon})}{R}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:normalized-geometry}
\end{equation}
Thus $N_Q>N_L$ for $0<r<R$, with equality at both endpoints and the order reversed for $r>R$.
For any curve whose interventions all stay in range, nonnegative integration weights preserve this weak ordering. Monotonicity is unnecessary. The area inequality is strict only when an interior residual receives positive integration weight.
Overshoots can reverse the area ordering and must be retained rather than clipped. When $R=0$, neither normalization is defined.
Changing from $Q$ to $L$ therefore lowers both insertion and deletion AUC for an in-range curve.
Larger insertion AUC and smaller deletion AUC are often taken to indicate better ranking quality. Under those conventions, the same response change suggests opposite changes in quality even though the interventions are identical.
The historical calculation does not require the recorded actual endpoint to be an exact self-reference.
Instead, every saved curve ordinate is transformed, its endpoints are recomputed under the new response, and it is integrated again on the same saved grid.
Duplicate handling leaves \RescoreCalls{} calls in \RescoreEpisodes{} episodes for each ranking cohort.
For quadratic-target vision rankings, the median deletion AUC drops from \RescoreQDeletionQ{} under $Q$ to \RescoreQDeletionL{} under $L$.
For stabilized-$\ell_2$ rankings, it rises from \RescoreLDeletionL{} under $L$ to \RescoreLDeletionQ{} under $Q$.
Table~\ref{tab:targets} and Figure~\ref{fig:response-geometry} report both modalities and both perturbation directions.

\subsection{An Analytic Random-Order Counterexample}
\label{sec:random-null}

As an analytic control, consider the finite additive scalar policy $f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$ at an all-one input, a zero baseline and the self-reference $f(x)=n$.
Every feature ordering gives the same perturbation values.
Whole-feature prefix interventions realize only the fractions $q=k/n$, with $k=0,\ldots,n$. The normalized quadratic deletion and insertion responses at those fractions are $1-q^2$ and $1-(1-q)^2$.
Trapezoidal integration over all $n+1$ prefix fractions gives
\[
\operatorname{AUC}_{n}=\frac23-\frac{1}{6n^2}
\]
for either curve.
Extending these values as polynomials and integrating continuously over $q\in[0,1]$ gives $2/3$. This is also the $n\to\infty$ limit, but it is not the exact finite-grid area.
With $n=100$, every fraction on the historical nine-point requested grid is realized exactly. Trapezoidal integration gives \RandomGridAUC{} for both curves under every ordering.
This finite construction rules out a universal random-order AUC of 0.5.
For comparison, the unstabilized negative-norm response is linear on the same fractions and has trapezoidal AUC 0.5.
A fixed reference such as 0.5 therefore needs justification for the particular policy, response and intervention protocol.
This is an analytic construction, not an empirical measurement of random-ranking performance on RDT.

\section{Numerical Diagnostics from an Incomplete Campaign}
\label{sec:numerical-validation}

The later campaign checks identified current computations separately from the historical records above.
Historical runs were rescored arithmetically using saved curves, but their original contexts could not be recovered. The later probe instead evaluates a separately identified offline fp32 function on authenticated contexts with its own reference actions.
This function cannot validate the historical attribution maps or provide a paired comparison with them.
The campaign was limited by compute cost and stopped before its planned numerical coverage and independent verification were complete.
The analysis uses a fixed, authenticated snapshot of sealed results. Entries that were incomplete at the snapshot are neither successful nor failed calculations.
The stopping decision supplied no statistical sampling rule for the completed subset. Passed and violated criteria therefore describe only that subset, not population failure rates.

\paragraph{Functions and precision controls.}
Stored observations, masks, reference actions and exact noise arrays provide fixed, authenticated inputs for comparison.
The diagnostic implementation separates accumulation arithmetic, master-path and final-product arithmetic, quadrature, and downstream model precision.
It compares full coordinate arrays, ranks over eligible groups, selected groups and perturbation responses.
The earlier downstream-fp32 factor control lifts the same bf16-rounded parameters and cached adapted representations. It also converts non-parameter dtype attributes explicitly while holding the original reference action fixed.
This changes downstream arithmetic while preserving the numeric inputs. It does not recover unrounded checkpoint parameters or test fp32 encoders.
Reusing the original reference also distinguishes this control from the later probe.
These earlier comparisons preceded the full repeated-gradient checks, so they cannot by themselves establish a globally repeatable precision contrast.

The later engineering campaign defines its own deterministic fp32 function. Image and language representations retain cached bf16 values lifted to fp32, raw state is re-adapted in fp32, and the reference action is the function's own fp32 prediction.
The function uses contexts visited by the bf16 policy and the stored source noise.
It is an offline intervention function, not the bf16 behavior policy or a derivative of that policy's executed commands.
Repeating endpoint outputs checks a different property from repeating path gradients and attribution maps.
Differences caused by changing the function or its reference therefore cannot be attributed to quadrature alone.

The analysis therefore uses fixed engineering call indices rather than a uniform sample of calls from complete trajectories.
Episodes were not selected by success or action norm.
The integration ladders use trapezoidal quadrature with fp32 path and accumulation arithmetic.
The same gradient stream produces a mean path-gradient map without IG's input-difference multiplier. This is a numerical diagnostic, not an evaluated efficacy baseline.
The protocol compares coordinates, ranks, completeness and common perturbation responses across integration budgets and repeated calculations.
Its tolerances diagnose changes in the tested approximation. They are neither efficacy margins nor calibrated error bounds relative to an exact attribution.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

We separate three questions: what the chosen scalar response measures, whether a numerical attribution is reliable, and whether its ranking contains useful information that depends on the model.
Rescoring fixed recorded interventions addresses the first question because the response changes while the selected features stay fixed.
It does not by itself establish numerical reliability or a useful model-dependent ranking.
Our claims concern exact relationships and measurements reconstructed from the records. The numerical diagnostics apply only to their stated coverage.
Establishing ranking efficacy would require a new paired study under common responses with informative controls. The present results do not establish it.
The elementary identities show which operation changes a reported evaluation. They help avoid interpreting a response change as an empirical improvement in ranking, without proposing a new general theory of attribution.
A common evaluation response defines the quantity being compared, but it is not a universal measure of explanation quality.
Its action coordinates, units, baselines, perturbation groups and aggregation must be justified for the intended use.
Matched random, input-difference and path-gradient controls would help separate information in the attribution from simple input structure and the IG multiplier.
The protocols for these comparisons are deferred. They provide no comparative or learned-weight-specific result in this paper.

\paragraph{A practical evaluation procedure.}
We recommend four steps when reporting action-discrepancy attributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item State the scalar attribution target and evaluation response separately. Also specify the reference action, fixed noise, feature representation, baseline, intervention groups and schedule.
\item Compare rankings on authenticated matched contexts under each common response. Claims of efficacy need matched random and simple ranking controls. To assess a response change, rescore the same interventions, recompute the endpoint normalization and retain overshoots and undefined gaps.
\item Assess repeatability and sensitivity of coordinates, rankings and responses across numerical settings on a declared context roster. Report failures and incomplete cases. Completeness and agreement on finitely many grids are numerical checks. They do not certify exact IG.
\item Declare the unit of analysis and dependence grouping. Keep response sensitivity, numerical reliability and ranking quality distinct, because none alone establishes improved closed-loop behavior.
\end{enumerate}
These steps make clear what each comparison identifies. They do not determine a universally adequate integration budget or evaluation response.

The historical records contain several sources of uncertainty, and each needs different treatment.
Source-record hashes verify the released bytes, but exact checkpoint and context identities can remain unknown.
Resampling whole episodes addresses dependence within episodes conditional on the recorded empirical mixture. It cannot repair shared or overwritten contexts, preprocessing confounds or missing training replicates.
The released 1B results come from one checkpoint. The solver and alternative-target analyses cover fewer tasks than the verification campaign.
Endpoint normalization permits unbounded response curves, so the results can be sensitive to endpoint gaps.
Camera-token and language-token interventions differ in their group counts and meaning. Their AUCs therefore cannot be used interchangeably as measures of modality importance.

The overlay panels are retained as historical artifacts, not as evidence of successful grounding or as diagnoses of behavioral failure.
The groups previously named from completeness residuals also describe numerical diagnostics. They are not independently validated labels for outcomes such as misgrounding or delayed credit.
The original main-pass and early budget-extension numbers could not be recovered and are therefore absent from the revised primary tables.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-budget} reports only later budget cohorts that can be reconstructed. Those cohorts do not establish convergence.

\section{Conclusion}

An explicit auxiliary action-discrepancy score defines per-policy-call IG for a fixed-noise diffusion sampler.
Across the saved RDT perturbation curves, changing only the evaluation response changes normalized AUC while keeping the selected features fixed.
All eight reported paired cases have positive median changes, whereas overshooting tails make six means negative. Both the summary statistic and endpoint geometry therefore shape the conclusion.
The finite-grid analytic counterexample rules out a universal random-ranking AUC of 0.5.
The canonical retrospective tables keep measurements supported by the available records and exclude primary constants that could not be recovered.
The actual-model diagnostics retain unresolved numerical failures and incomplete coverage.
The smooth aliasing construction shows that finite grids can agree while producing an ordering different from exact IG.
A common evaluation response is needed to interpret a comparison of ranking quality. These results do not show that one target or attribution method performs better under that response.

\section{Response-Geometry Derivations}
\label{sec:geometry-proofs}

The identities in Section~\ref{sec:path-weights} keep the action reference fixed and assume that the relevant path gradients are integrable.
Differentiating $Q=-r/(2D\sigma^2)$ and $L=-\sqrt{r+\epsilon}$ gives Equation~\ref{eq:gradient-weight}.
To obtain Equation~\ref{eq:normalized-geometry}, set $a_0=\sqrt\epsilon$, $b=\sqrt{R+\epsilon}$ and $z=\sqrt{r+\epsilon}$.
Then $R=b^2-a_0^2$ and $r=z^2-a_0^2$, so rationalization gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{b^2-z^2}{R}-\frac{b-z}{b-a_0}
=\frac{(z-a_0)(b-z)}{R}.
\end{equation}
For in-range residuals, the maximum difference is $(b-a_0)^2/(4R)\le1/4$.
This pointwise derivation applies to interventions from a fixed ranking. It does not order different rankings or establish improvements in task success.

For the rank-reversal example, take $c=4/5$ and parameterize the path by $(t,t)$.
The scalar residual is $d(t)=1+c-t^2-ct=(1-t)(1+t+c)\ge0$.
The quadratic path integrands are $2td(t)$ and $cd(t)$, giving
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}^{Q}=\left(\frac12+\frac c3,\frac{2c}3+\frac{c^2}2\right)
=\left(\frac{23}{30},\frac{64}{75}\right).
\end{equation}
For the stabilized negative norm, the path integrands are $2t\,d(t)/\sqrt{d(t)^2+\epsilon}$ and $c\,d(t)/\sqrt{d(t)^2+\epsilon}$.
Using the bound $d(t)\ge c(1-t)$ and monotonicity of $1-d/\sqrt{d^2+\epsilon}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\int_0^1\left(1-\frac{d(t)}{\sqrt{d(t)^2+\epsilon}}\right)dt
\le1-\frac{\sqrt{c^2+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon}{c}
\le\frac{\sqrt\epsilon}{c}.
\end{equation}
At the implemented stabilizer, this gives $0\le1-\mathrm{IG}^{L}_1\le2\sqrt\epsilon/c=2.5\times10^{-6}$ and $0\le c-\mathrm{IG}^{L}_2\le\sqrt\epsilon=10^{-6}$.
All coordinates are positive. The stabilized $L$ margin is at least $0.1999975$, while $Q$ has the reverse ordering with exact margin $13/150$.
The artifact checks these identities through independent differentiation, integration and normalization. The checks include overshoots, zero gaps and a policy example without rank reversal.

All four negative insertion means stay negative under every individual
omission. The Q language-deletion mean changes sign in 3 of 30 omissions,

not a share of the signed net mean. It does not justify excluding that episode.

Endpoint normalization helps explain the extreme vision-insertion changes.
For the most negative Q-ranking call, the baseline squared residual is
$0.0174195$, the smallest value among its 750-call cohort. The same call's
maximum saved perturbed residual is $653.053$, a ratio of about $37{,}490$.
